This masterclass is about how to embody the highest, best version of yourself, even when it's very challenging to do so.

Why ethics aren't optional

I want to be absolutely clear here. You will almost undoubtedly have a moment in your career where you are faced with an ethical dilemma. In fact you may have many of these moments in your career. The higher you go, the more things like ethics tend to take a back seat to other objectives of the people around you.

What are business ethics?

Business ethics are about going beyond legal requirements in order to include moral principles, values, and how decisions impact all stakeholders (including employees, customers, vendors, the community, the environment, etc.) Ethics is about doing the right thing even when no one is watching, and especially when the stakes are high. Leaders who show up as ethical build trust with their teams. Making ethical decisions that hurt the shareholder value in the short term tend to improve shareholder value in the long-term, because of the follow-on benefits that come from a team trusting their leadership more.

Individual decisions, collective impact

One ethical or unethical decision can have far-reaching consequences. They can impact your reputation, the reputation of the organization, financial stability, employee morale, and even public perception. Sometimes leaders can feel 'ethical fatigue', which is a state of weariness resulting from the constant need to make ethical decisions and navigate complex moral dilemmas. Leaders can feel drained and depleted by the effort of trying to do what's right when it would just be so much easier to do the wrong thing "just this once". True leaders understand their decisions echo throughout an organization, and even beyond. Your personal ethical compass directly shapes the organization's culture and morals.

Identifying common ethical blind spots

The most common places where ethical dilemmas appear are when there is a conflict of interest between the shareholders and other stakeholders (such as employees or customers). Whenever financial gain and quality of delivery are standing face to face, moral ambiguity can arise. You'll also find ethical blind spots when the risk to being discovered is low relative to the potential reward an individual would gain by doing something unethical. For example: hitting a six-figure bonus target by slightly skewing the numbers in a proposal to a customer.

Trusting your inner executive

This is why it's so important to know yourself and know your values. This is why the Foundations module is so critical to being a great executive leader. In times of moral ambiguity, leaning on your own sense of what is right and wrong can be a guide through the darkness.

The power of personal values

I encourage you to identify your core personal values. Do you believe in integrity, honesty, fairness, respect, accountability? Why or why not? These values serve as an internal compass, guiding your decisions when external rules are either unclear or possibly even conflicting one another. Your personal values are your non-negotiables. Understanding them deeply provides you with the clarity you need in ambiguous situations.

Proactively spotting ethical crossroads

Sometimes you can spot a moral dilemma a mile away. Try to anticipate potential ethical dilemmas that you or your team might face before you actually have to react to them. What training can you provide to your team (and to yourself) on how to handle potential dilemmas like this? You can use simple frameworks to help everyone (and yourself) know what rules to play by. For example:

  • The Golden Rule: do unto others as you would have them do unto you.
  • The Transparency Test: would this look good on the front page of the news?
  • The Stakeholder Test: does this create a win-lose scenario between any of the key stakeholders such as shareholders, employees, customers, vendors, the community or the environment?

A leader's strength exists in their ability to see around corners, not just react to what's directly in front of them. This applies to ethical foresight.

Your inner voice

Have you ever just "felt" a decision you had to make? Like, it was a full body "feeling" that compelled you to decide one way or another? That is your instinct, intuition, inner voice, whatever you'd like to call it. That voice is a combination of your conscious and subconscious beliefs and biases telling you what is the right thing to do in a situation. Listen to that inner voice. If you get a "gut feeling" or some sort of "internal alarm" when a situation feels "off", that discomfort is your ethical judgment speaking to you. And don't let imposter syndrome override your sensibility. Remember: your unique perspective and moral compass is invaluable, don't dismiss your intuition, especially in areas of right and wrong.

When doing right means standing alone

The price of integrity

Let's acknowledge the real-world pressures we face as executives:

  • The fear of losing your job (and income, and prestige)
  • Career stagnation
  • Social ostracization in your company or industry
  • Financial repercussions
  • Being seen as "difficult"

The reality is that many people fail to measure up to their own standard in ethical dilemmas. When you DO measure up, stand up, do what is right even if it is hard, that's when you can claim the mantle of integrity as yours. The near-term price of doing the right thing yields a long-term ROI of true leadership, distinguishing you as an impactful leader, separate from those who just go along with the strongest voice around them.

How to stand up effectively

So what do you actually DO when you're faced with a moral dilemma? Here are some steps:

  • Preparation: document your concerns. Make sure you understand the company policies. Seek confidential counsel from HR, legal, or trusted mentors in your organization.
  • Communication: learn how to articulate the ethical concern you see both clearly and professionally. Focus on the principles of the organization that are at risk, as well as the potential negative consequences of being discovered.
  • Build alliances: see if you can identify allies who would share your ethical stance, and enlist them to help you in changing things for the better.
  • Know your "red line": most importantly, you need to understand what ethical compromises you will never make. Be prepared to walk away if necessary.

The indelible value of your integrity

While immediate career impacts can be daunting to consider, remember that the long-term benefits of maintaining your integrity are immeasurable:

  • Unshakeable self-respect
  • Peace of mind, the ability to sleep well at night with a clear conscience
  • Genuine respect and enhanced reputation among peers and future employers
  • The ability to truly lead with authenticity and conviction
  • Attracting like-minded ethical professionals who want to work with/for you

Final thought

Your career is a journey, but your character is your foundation. Choosing integrity, even at a cost, is not a sacrifice; it is an investment in the most powerful version of yourself, the executive leader who inspires through unwavering principle.
