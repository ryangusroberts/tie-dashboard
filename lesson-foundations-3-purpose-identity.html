Goal for this lesson: make the mindset shifts that unlock the next level, and find the purpose that keeps you aligned whatever your dial. Identity precedes income.

The secret hardly anyone believes at first

If you believe you're the right person to lead, and you understand what leading actually entails, it matters less how much raw skill or experience you have. There's a minimum level of skill required, and most people at the manager or director level already have it. What they lack is the mindset. That's why people get frustrated watching less-experienced colleagues get promoted: they're playing the wrong game, optimizing for the wrong things.

The six myths of executive leadership

These are the six mindset shifts that unlock the next level:

  1. Executives are not just higher-level managers. Executives focus outward; managers focus inward. Executives look to the future; managers track the past. Executives decide the right things to do; managers do things the right way.
  2. Working hard doesn't get you promoted to executive. Volume of work isn't what's required at the next level. Your ability to do one thing does not scale into executive-minded leadership. Working really hard can actually make you unpromotable.
  3. MBAs aren't the solution you think. An MBA teaches business basics. If you already have those, it becomes a networking play, and the ROI versus building your brand and skills is poor.
  4. Professional certifications aren't either. Certs demonstrate professional ability, not executive ability. In most cases, invest in your brand and skills over a badge.
  5. It's about their future, not your past. In an interview, spend 30 seconds on your highlights, then two minutes on what you can do for the organization. They want to know what you're like to work with. Show up authentically.
  6. Executives need a strong personal brand. Your brand is what people say about you when you're not in the room. If you don't brand yourself, someone else will. Your brand is how you get opportunities designed for you to succeed.

The three core traits of a star operator

Three traits matter most, whether you're leading a team or building your own practice:

TraitWhat it means
AuthenticityBeing your true self at all times, which requires actually knowing yourself: your beliefs, fears, dreams, morals, and values.
EmpathyCommunicating effectively with others. If you can't empathize with someone's needs, they won't willingly follow you.
AudacityBeing willing to go after big goals, take on massive projects, and work outside your comfort zone to build great things.

If you're authentic, empathetic to others, and audacious enough to build big things, you're a fit for what we teach.

Scarcity to abundance

In scarcity, we believe resources are finite, money, time, promotions, so we hoard, protect, and become self-preserving. Scarcity thrives on fear: fear of missing out, of not being enough, of failure. It leads to risk aversion and protecting what you have instead of growing.

In abundance, we make room for our future, growth, and opportunities. Opportunities aren't finite. An abundance mindset lets you take calculated risks, celebrate others' wins, and see challenges as chances to learn. You begin to see the world in terms of "we" instead of "me," and that's exactly the leader people want to work for and follow.

How to make the shift

  • Challenge your limiting beliefs. Ask: "Is this really true? What tangible evidence supports it?"
  • Practice gratitude. Your brain cannot feel fear and gratitude at the same time. Regularly appreciating the good shifts your focus from what's lacking to what you have.
  • Focus on giving. When you feel down or anxious, look for a way to help someone. It reframes you as a giver.
  • Celebrate your success. You're likely already where you once dreamed of being. Try celebrating your own achievements and those of others around you. It will remind you that success is possible for everyone.
  • Focus on giving. Anytime you feel down, depressed, or anxious, look for opportunities to help others. Whether it's sharing your knowledge, offering support, or simply lending a shoulder to someone to share their burden, it really does help reframe yourself as a giver.

Journaling exercise

Get into the habit of journaling your thoughts about your career, yourself, and your life journey. It's a great way to work through roadblocks and big ideas, and to reflect on how far you have come as a person. Here are some prompts.

Prompts for executive mindset:

  • Authenticity: "How can I show up more authentically in my interactions at work? What masks or personas am I ready to shed?"
  • Empathy: "How can I deepen my empathy for my colleagues and their perspectives? How can I better understand their needs and motivations?"
  • Audacity: "What bold goals or ambitious projects am I ready to pursue? What steps can I take to move outside my comfort zone?"

Prompts to challenge limiting beliefs:

  • Identify scarcity thinking: "What are three specific examples of how scarcity thinking shows up in my work or life? How does this hold me back?"
  • Uncover underlying beliefs: "What are some deep-seated beliefs I have about resources, opportunities, or my own potential? Where did these beliefs originate?"
  • Reframe limiting beliefs: "Choose one limiting belief identified above. How can I reframe this belief to reflect an abundance mindset?"

Prompts to practice gratitude:

  • Daily gratitude practice: "What are three things I am grateful for today? How do these things enrich my life?"
  • Gratitude for challenges: "Reflect on a recent challenge or setback. What lessons or opportunities for growth did this experience provide?"
  • Gratitude for others: "Who are the people I am most grateful for in my life? How have they supported me and contributed to my success?"

Ikigai: your reason for getting up

Ikigai (ee-kee-guy) is a Japanese philosophy: a motivating force, something that gives you a sense of purpose. It rests on four elements, and their overlap is your reason for being.

What you love: your passions, whether or not they're profitable. What you're good at: the skills you've built, not just the ones you're paid for. What the world needs: the missions and problems that spark your energy. What you can be paid for: how your focus and passion arrange into a viable career or offer.

Many of us fill two or three of the four. Missing just one causes trouble: be good at something, paid for it, and needed by the world, but not love it, and you'll be comfortable and empty.

For example, when you are good at something, can be paid for it, and it is what the world needs, but you do not love what you do, you will be comfortable but have a feeling of emptiness. Consider your current role. Do you love what you do? Are you good at it? What is missing in your own Venn diagram?

Tom's Ikigai

It took a long time to fully integrate this Life Purpose Venn Diagram. Tom spent 25 years in a career he loved at first, but fell out of love with 10 years on. He had convinced himself the world needed what he did after 9/11, but after years in the Defense industry, he began to question whether the world really needed weaponized communications technology. Only during a cancer journey did he realize another life purpose. After several iterations:

  • What I love: I love mentoring and coaching
  • What I am good at: I am good at connecting deeply with people
  • What the world needs: The world needs a whole generation of better leaders
  • What I can be paid for: I can be paid for coaching programs
  • My Passion: Teaching and mentoring through deep connections
  • My Mission: To mentor a whole generation of authentic, empathetic, and audacious leaders
  • My Vocation: Leading a coaching program for authentic, empathetic and audacious leaders
  • My Profession: Building coaching programs that allow me to connect deeply with clients
Tom's Life Purpose / Ikigai: "Mentoring a new generation of leaders by connecting deeply with our clients through our leadership coaching programs."

Activity: find your Ikigai

Find a quiet place and set aside some time to reflect on these four elements. Write the name of the element at the top of a blank page, so four pages for all four elements. Then, on each page, fill in as many things as you can.

Do not try to link them yet. This is critically important for the exercise to work. Our goal is to expand your vision beyond the myopic view you have from your current situation. When you think about things you love doing, do not think about whether they help the world or not. When you think about things you are good at, do not concern yourself with whether you can be paid for them. And so on. Once you have recorded several thoughts on each page, now see what you can link together. Get creative. You may surprise yourself with what comes up for you. There are thousands of ways to make money doing something you love that you have never considered before.

Prompting questions

Sometimes the hardest part of filling in an Ikigai diagram is figuring out what questions to ask yourself. Below are prompting questions that might help spur some thinking.

Question 1: What do you love?
  • What do you never get bored of?
  • What makes you smile?
  • When do you feel happiest?
  • What 5 things do you cherish most?
  • What were you doing when you last lost track of time?
  • In the past, what has left you feeling energized?
  • Who are the people you love spending time with?
  • What did you love to do as a child?
  • If you were financially secure, what would you do with your time?
  • Is there an activity, person, or place that you never get bored of?
  • What activities make you lose track of time?
  • What aspects of your life make you feel alive and energized?
  • What do you feel passionate about?
  • What are your values, and how do you use them every day?
  • Where do you find beauty?
  • If you could be the best at one thing, what would it be?
  • What can you not stop talking about outside of the workplace?
  • What would you continue to do even if you did not get paid?
  • What would you do with your time if you didn't have to worry about making money?
  • How would you spend your perfect weekend?
  • What excites you when you do it?
  • What could you talk about for hours on end?
Question 2: What are you good at?
  • What skills do you spend time practicing?
  • What do people approach you for help with?
  • Have you ever won an award?
  • What skills do you list on your resume?
  • Is there something you would like to be good at?
  • To what activities have you ever devoted over 100 hours practicing or learning?
  • If you had to teach something, what would you teach?
  • What skills or talents come naturally to you?
  • What do you excel at even when you are not trying?
  • What makes you feel good about yourself?
  • What do other people compliment you about?
  • What achievements are you proud of?
  • Do you get excited about developing your skills in this area further?
  • What parts of your current job come to you easily?
  • In what activity do you excel in your social circle, workplace, or community?
  • With more education and experience, could you be among the best at what you do?
Question 3: What can you be paid to do?
  • What have you been paid for before?
  • What would you be doing if you were not in your current job?
  • What do people need? Do you think they would be happy to pay for it?
  • Can doing this give you the income you want/need right now?
  • Can you make a good living doing this work in the long term?
  • Are people willing to part with their resources to pay for what you have to offer?
  • What does the competition look like, can you spot a niche?
  • Which jobs, positions, or tasks spark your interest?
  • Are you already making a good living in your line of work?
Question 4: What does the world need?
  • What do the people in your immediate society need?
  • Is there something about the way the world works that you want to change?
  • How can you contribute positively to those around you?
  • Think of 3 skills you have that are in high demand.
  • What changes would you like to bring about in the world?
  • What can you do or offer that would bring meaning to others?
  • If you could get a message across to a large group of people, what would it be?
  • How do you help others?
  • What can you teach others?
  • What kind of contribution would you love to make in life?
  • What problems in your society would you like to help solve?
  • What issues in your community or the whole world touch you emotionally?
  • Will your work still be relevant a decade from now?
  • What would you like to see people do more of or differently?
  • What is the world lacking?
  • How could you be more involved in your community?

Your four components, then your statement

Start with these four lists. Fill in as many as you can. Don't hold back no matter how unrelated or silly your logical mind may tell you these things are. Next, begin integrating them two at a time: when you do what you love and what the world needs, you define your Mission. Finally, put it all together into a Life Purpose Statement that integrates the best components. It's okay to try a few out. Sometimes just verbalizing multiple ideas to someone else helps crystallize which one resonates the strongest.
