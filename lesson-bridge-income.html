Bridge Income

Stand up one lean fractional or consulting offer, fast, to cover the gap and prove your value.

What you'll learn: why bridge income is about leverage more than money, how to package one lean fractional or consulting offer in a week, how to price it, and how to land the first one or two clients from the network you already have.

Here's the mindset shift that makes the whole Bridge track work: bridge income is not a downgrade, it's leverage. The moment you have even one client paying you directly for your expertise, three things change. Your runway extends. Your fear drops, which makes you a far better interviewer and negotiator. And you now have a real BATNA, a Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement, sitting behind every conversation with a potential employer. You are no longer a person who needs a job. You are an independent operator who might take the right one.

That is the entire reason the cross-leverage rule exists in this program. On the Bridge, you're not building a starter asset in the other lane, you're running both lanes for real. The offer you stand up here is the same offer someone in the Build track builds, just leaner and faster, because your goal right now is income and dignity, not a scaled business. If it grows into the business, wonderful. That's Bridge B5, Decide. For now, we just need money coming in the door and proof that people will pay you.

Lean, not perfect

The Build track teaches you to architect an offer carefully, validate it in dozens of conversations, and build product-market fit. That is the right approach when independence is your destination. On the Bridge, you don't have that kind of time, and you don't need that level of polish. You need one offer, aimed at people who already know you're good, that solves a problem they already have. Speed beats perfection here. You will refine it later if you decide to keep going.

Four boxes. If you can fill these in, you have an offer you can sell this week.

Step 1: Pick the offer you can sell fastest

Go back to your Zone of Value from Foundations. Somewhere in there is a thing you do that people have already paid for, asked you about, or thanked you for. That's your bridge offer. Don't overthink it. The best bridge offer is usually a slice of the exact work you were just doing in your last role, sold to the kind of company that has that exact problem. A few common shapes:

  • Fractional leadership: a few days a month as a fractional CxO, VP, or head of function for a company too small to hire you full-time but too big to go without your seniority.
  • Advisory / consulting retainer: a monthly retainer where you're on call for strategy, reviews, and key decisions.
  • A defined project: a specific, scoped engagement with a start and an end, a turnaround, a go-to-market plan, a systems overhaul, a diligence review.
  • Coaching or interim work: mentoring a rising leader, or stepping in to run a function during a gap on their side.

Step 2: Package it in four sentences

You are not building a website or a brand this week. You are writing four sentences you can say on a call or paste into a message. Fill in the four boxes:

Who: I help [specific kind of company or leader]...

What: ...who are struggling with [specific problem you're known for solving]...

Outcome: ...get to [specific, concrete result] within [timeframe]...

How/Price: ...through [fractional days / a retainer / a scoped project] at [a simple number].

That's the offer. If you can say it without stumbling and the person nods, you're in business. If their eyes glaze, it's too vague, narrow it until it's sharp.

Step 3: Price it simply and without apology

Executives new to selling their own time almost always price too low out of fear. Don't. You are selling senior expertise, and the buyer is paying for judgment, not hours. A few ways to anchor your number:

  • From your old salary: a rough starting point for a full day of fractional work is your former daily rate (salary plus benefits divided by working days), then marked up, because they get none of the overhead, benefits, or long-term commitment of an employee, and all of the expertise.
  • From the outcome: if your work saves or makes them a meaningful figure, your fee should be a small, obvious fraction of that value. Price the result, not the clock.
  • Retainers over hourly: a monthly retainer (a fixed fee for a defined scope of access and deliverables) is cleaner, more predictable for your runway, and keeps you out of the trap of justifying every hour.

Pick a number that feels slightly uncomfortable and say it calmly. Silence after the number is your friend. Let them respond.

This isn't tax or financial advice, but do set money aside for taxes from your very first invoice, and if you're doing this seriously, ask an accountant about the right structure to invoice through. Getting this right early saves a painful surprise later.

Step 4: Land the first one or two from your warm network

You do not do cold outreach for bridge income. You go to the people who already know you're good. Speed comes from warmth. Make a list of everyone who has, in the last few years, worked with you, hired you, reported to you, sold to you, or told you "let me know if you ever go out on your own." That list is your pipeline.

The move is not "I'm looking for a job" and it's not "please hire my new company." It's the advice-and-value approach you already learned in the Career track's Outreach lesson, aimed at work instead of a role:

Hi [name], I've decided to take on a small number of [fractional / advisory] clients while I consider my next move, and you were one of the first people I thought of. I'm helping [who] fix [what]. Even if it's not a fit for you, do you know anyone wrestling with that right now? Happy to be a resource either way.

Notice what that message does. It signals you're in demand (a small number of clients), it's specific enough to trigger a referral, and it gives them an easy way to help even if they can't hire you. This is the one-give-one-ask principle from the networking lessons: lead with being useful, and the work follows.

Mind your non-solicit here. If your agreement restricts contacting former clients or colleagues, respect it, work your broader network instead, and refer back to Stabilize on getting the language reviewed.

Step 5: Deliver like an executive, get the proof

When you land the first engagement, over-deliver on the outcome and, just as importantly, capture the proof. A short testimonial, a concrete result you can quote, a reference who'll take a call. That proof does double duty: it makes the next bridge client easier to land, and it makes you a more compelling candidate in your search, because now you're the executive who went out, got hired directly on your expertise, and delivered. That story wins interviews.

Do this now

  • Write your four-sentence offer: who, what, outcome, price.
  • Set your number and practice saying it out loud, calmly, three times.
  • Build your warm list: everyone who already knows you're good.
  • Send the first five messages this week.
  • Bring your offer to a group coaching call and let us pressure-test it.
