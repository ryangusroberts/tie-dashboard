Stabilize

Runway, health coverage, non-competes, and the plan, so you're deciding from calm, not panic.

What you'll learn: how to buy yourself calm before you make any big move, get your runway and coverage in order, understand the paperwork that follows you out the door, and write the one-page plan that turns a scary gap into a managed project.

The Bridge is where most executives arrive in a panic. Either the role ended, or you know it's ending, or you've decided you cannot stay one more quarter. Whatever brought you here, the single most valuable thing you can do first is not to sprint. It's to stabilize. The people who make bad career decisions almost always make them from a place of fear, scarcity, and time pressure. Our whole goal in this lesson is to take those three things off the table so that every decision you make from here comes from a calm, clear, powered-up version of you.

Remember the Income Dial. The Bridge is the middle position: you are between roles, and you are going to run two lanes at once, income and dignity in the near term, and a real search or build in the mid term. But none of that works if you're underwater and terrified. So we stabilize first.

The three things that create panic

Name the fear, then hand it its antidote. Panic is just three unanswered questions.

1. Know your Freedom Number and your runway

If you completed the Freedom Number lesson in Foundations, pull it back up now, because this is the moment it was built for. If you skipped it, go do it first. Your Freedom Number is the amount of money you need each month to keep your life running, split into two versions: your bare-bones number (what it costs to keep the lights on, the family fed, and the essential bills paid) and your full-life number (what it costs to live the way you actually want to live).

Your runway is simple math: the cash and liquid resources you can access, divided by your bare-bones monthly number. That gives you the number of months you can go with zero new income. Write it down. It is almost always longer than the panicked voice in your head is telling you, and seeing the real number on paper is the first thing that lowers the temperature.

Cash you can actually reach, over what it truly costs to keep the essentials running.

Extend the runway before you need to

  • Trim to bare-bones now, not later. Every discretionary subscription, upgrade, and nice-to-have you pause today buys you more months of calm. You can turn them back on the day you sign.
  • Know what you can access without penalty. Emergency savings, a taxable brokerage account, a HELOC you already have open. Understand the order you'd draw on things, and the tax or penalty cost of each, before you're forced to decide in a hurry.
  • Account for what's owed to you. Unpaid PTO, a final paycheck, a bonus you're still eligible for, unvested equity with a cliff coming up. Some of these are negotiable on the way out (more on that below).
A note on the money: I'm not your financial advisor, and this isn't personalized financial advice. Before you tap retirement accounts, take on new debt, or make an irreversible money move, talk to a professional who knows your full picture. The goal here is to know your numbers, not to make tax decisions in a panic.

2. Solve health coverage deliberately

For a lot of executives, especially in the United States, the scariest part of a gap isn't the salary, it's the health insurance. Do not let this be a vague anxiety humming in the background. Make it a solved problem with a name and a date.

  • Understand your continuation options. When employer coverage ends, you typically have a window to continue it for a period at your own cost (in the US, this is COBRA). It's expensive, but it's a known bridge, and it keeps your doctors and your deductible progress intact.
  • Compare the marketplace. Losing job-based coverage is usually a qualifying life event that opens a special enrollment window. A marketplace plan is often cheaper than continuation, sometimes with subsidies depending on your income for the year.
  • Check a partner's plan. If you have a spouse or partner with coverage, your job loss may open an enrollment window on their plan too. This is frequently the cheapest and simplest option and people forget it exists.
  • Mind the gap dates. The single mistake to avoid is an accidental lapse. Map the exact date your current coverage ends and the exact date the next coverage begins, and make sure they touch.
Same caveat: benefits rules change and depend on where you live and your situation. Confirm the specifics with the plan administrator, a licensed broker (many marketplace brokers are free to you), or your partner's HR before you rely on any of it.

3. Understand the paperwork that follows you out the door

When you leave a role, a few documents you signed years ago suddenly matter a great deal. You want to read them before you make a plan, not after you've accidentally violated one.

  • Non-compete. Does your agreement restrict where you can work or what you can do, for how long, and in what geography or industry? Enforceability varies enormously by location and has been changing, so don't assume it's ironclad and don't assume it's toothless. Read the actual language.
  • Non-solicitation. This usually matters more in practice than the non-compete. Can you contact former clients? Former colleagues? For how long? This directly shapes how you can stand up bridge income, so know it cold.
  • Confidentiality & IP. What information and work product belongs to them? As covered in the Career track, copy anything proprietary back to them and off your personal devices, and remember your personal relationships and your contact list are yours, but a customer database is not.
  • Severance & separation agreements. If you're offered one, it is very often negotiable, and signing it may waive rights. This is exactly where a short conversation with an employment attorney pays for itself many times over.
I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice. Restrictive covenants are genuinely situation-specific and jurisdiction-specific. If money, a non-compete, or a separation agreement is in play, have an employment attorney read it before you sign anything or launch an offer that might brush up against it. It is one of the highest-ROI hours you'll spend.

4. Negotiate the exit while you still have leverage

Your leverage is highest on the way out, before you've signed the separation paperwork. Whether you're being let go or leaving on your own terms, there are often things worth asking for, calmly and professionally:

  • Severance, or more of it, and how it's paid.
  • An extension of health coverage or a subsidy toward it.
  • Accelerated or extended equity vesting.
  • Agreement on the reference and the story: what will they say when someone calls?
  • Keeping your laptop or phone number, extended email forwarding, or outplacement support.
  • A carve-out or written clarification on the non-compete or non-solicit for the specific bridge work you intend to do.

You may not get all of it. You will almost certainly get more than you would have gotten by not asking. Everything you learned in the Negotiations lesson applies here: know your walk-away, anchor thoughtfully, and stay in the calm, unhurried executive presence that makes people want to help you.

5. Write the one-page plan

The last step in stabilizing is to turn the fog into a project. One page. It converts "I don't know what's going to happen to me" into "here is what I'm running." Fill in these lines:

My runway is ___ months at bare-bones, ___ months at full-life.

My health coverage is handled via ___, starting ___, at ___ per month.

My restrictions are: non-compete ___, non-solicit ___, confidentiality ___ (reviewed by ___ on ___).

My dial for the next 90 days points to: Bridge, running two lanes.

Lane 1 (income + dignity now): stand up one lean offer. Target first dollar by ___.

Lane 2 (the search): reactivate brand + network + interviews. Target first conversation by ___.

I will know I'm stable when ___.

That page is your command brief. When the anxious voice shows up at 2 a.m., you don't argue with it. You reread the page. The situation is handled, and you are running it.

Do this now

  • Calculate your runway in months, both bare-bones and full-life, and write it down.
  • Pick your health coverage path and the two dates that must touch.
  • Pull your agreements and read the non-compete, non-solicit, and confidentiality language. Flag anything for an attorney.
  • List what you'll ask for in the exit while you still have leverage.
  • Fill in the one-page plan and bring it to your next group coaching call.
