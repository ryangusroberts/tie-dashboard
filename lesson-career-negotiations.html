Negotiation starts at the application

One of the most important things to remember about negotiating your compensation for a new role is that the negotiation starts even before you submit your resume for an opportunity. The more brand equity you have online and in your industry, the more desirable you will be to the hiring team. Once you do speak with an HR professional about the role, they will almost certainly ask you for your compensation requirements. There are two approaches to answering this question:

  • Delay / avoid: "I would need to know a bit more about the role and responsibilities before I can understand what fair compensation would be. What is the company's salary range for this role?" Use this approach if you want to hold back on sharing your position. Usually you do this if they reach out to you first, or it's clear you are a star candidate.
  • Direct / candid: "My compensation range is between x and y." Use this approach if you already know the compensation range offered by the company, and choose a lower number near the top of their range.

The art of negotiation

One of the most critical skills that you will need as an executive leader is the ability to negotiate effectively. You're going to need to know when to cut your losses and when to go in for the kill. You'll need to have the ability to be aggressive, be patient, and be willing to walk away. Let's dive in to a bit on executive negotiation skills.

Key negotiation concepts

Why negotiation is important for executives

Negotiation is crucial for executives in:

  • Deal-making: mergers, acquisitions, partnerships, contracts.
  • Resource allocation: budgeting, staffing, project prioritization.
  • Conflict resolution: internal disputes, external disagreements.
  • Leadership: influencing teams, motivating stakeholders.
  • Compensation negotiations: for themselves and their teams.

Defining business negotiation

Negotiation is a process of reaching an agreement between two or more parties with different interests. One party needs something the other party has, and usually both need what each other has. The fair exchange of value has to be determined and agreed to by both sides of the deal, and this is where most deals fall apart.

BATNA & ZOPA

One of my favorite terms from my MBA program was this one, the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (a concept from Roger Fisher and William Ury's Getting to Yes). This is your walk-away point. What's the best you can do if no agreement is reached? A strong BATNA empowers you to be bold in your negotiations. A weak BATNA can lead to a significant loss in value along the way. For example, having three job offers on the table at one time gives you a strong BATNA, you can negotiate with the deal you want the most while knowing you'll still have a new job somewhere else. A weak BATNA might look like being on the outside of an airlock running out of oxygen, trying to convince a stubborn computer to open the doors.

The Zone of Possible Agreement is the range where the two sides overlap in terms of perceived value. The settlement ranges are what each party believes would be the best outcome and the worst outcome to make the deal work. Beyond the worst outcome is the BATNA for each party.

The ZOPA is where both settlement ranges overlap. Beyond each party's worst acceptable outcome lies their BATNA, the walk-away alternative.

Negotiation styles

This is where your personality, patience, and experience can really help you be successful in business, or keep you stuck.

Win-lose approach (distributive)

Focusing on maximizing one's own gains, oftentimes at the expense of the other party. This is an effective one-time, transactional negotiation, but you will burn all good will with the other party by 'running up the score' on them. This is usually reserved for time-sensitive negotiations involving scarce resources, like price gouging.

Win-win approach (integrative)

Almost certainly my favorite type of negotiation is when both parties gain more value than they give up. If you join this program and make 10x the cost of joining back in your new executive career, we both won in that case. Note that win-win deals require a high degree of trust and cooperation, and can be time-consuming. I have made an entire career on approaching things this way.

Compromising

This is about finding a middle ground where each party gives up something. This can be a quick way to reach an agreement, but may lead to less value on the table for everyone, and it doesn't always address the underlying needs of one or both parties. This is used when time doesn't allow for the win-win approach, or the two parties are at an impasse and any deal is better than no deal, or when a temporary solution is needed to hold things together or move forward.

Avoiding

Sometimes the only winning move is not to play. This can be appropriate if the issue is trivial or the relationship is not important, but it can damage relationships, and make the other party feel unheard. I tend to do this when emotions are running high or the cost of the negotiation outweighs the benefits.

Accommodating

This approach to negotiation is about yielding to the other party's demands. An unconditional surrender in war would be an example here. This works to preserve the relationship, especially if you were in the wrong in the first place. Be careful not to be manipulated into this frame of mind, people who negotiate in bad faith will try to put you here to get the most out of you (win-lose).

Negotiation tactics

There are elements of the negotiation process that can help you have a successful negotiation with another party. Preparation, patience, and persistence are paramount.

Preparation

This is the foundation of successful negotiation. Research: know the other party, their interests, and their BATNA. Set clear goals: what do you want to achieve? What are your must-haves vs. your nice-to-haves? Develop a strategy: how will you approach the negotiation?

Active listening

This is how to actually hear the needs and desires of the other party. Pay attention: focus on what they are saying, both verbally and nonverbally. Ask clarifying questions: ensure you understand their needs and concerns. Summarize: show that you are listening and understanding.

Framing

Present your position in a way that is persuasive to the other party. Focus on mutual gains: how will your proposal benefit them? Use data and evidence: support your claims with facts and figures. Control the narrative: don't let the other party frame the discussion in a way that disadvantages you.

Anchoring

Anchoring is setting the initial offer or expectation. When you go into a car dealership and see the list price for a car you want to buy, that is the anchored price. A well-placed anchor can influence the entire negotiation.

Building rapport

You should establish a connection with the other party. Find areas of mutual interest and belief, build a relationship based on trust and empathy. People are more likely to agree with (and help) people they know, like, and trust.

Handling difficult tactics

Sometimes the other party will attempt to manipulate the negotiation. Sometimes the other party is just a jerk.

  • Aggressive behavior: stay calm and assertive. Don't be afraid to walk away. Know your BATNA and don't go below it.
  • Lowball offers: counter with a realistic offer and explain your reasoning.
  • Ultimatums: these are designed to create Fear Of Missing Out (FOMO). Question the ultimatum and explore some alternatives. Don't be afraid to say no.
