Key Executive Skills, Part I

Vision and strategy, change management, and organizational design, the table-stakes skills of the executive level.

There are some table stakes to becoming an executive that involve skills that aren't readily developed at lower levels of the organization. Mastering these skills will help you drive growth in your organization, lead your teams effectively, and handle complex business challenges better.

The magic trick you have to perform now is the same as when you began your career: the Catch-22. You need to have these skills in order to get into a role where you can develop them.

So how do we do that? The Short Answer is to get exposure to these skills and experiences wherever you can, build your knowledge of how they work on your own, and constantly ask for opportunities to engage in these skills.

There's no way to learn every detail of every executive skill you need to know in a program like this, there's a reason MBAs take two years. That being said, this is a PRIMER on what you should know, and points you toward good knowledge around these topics. So let's dive in.

Vision & Strategy

Most people expect their executive leaders to have the ability to create and explain a vision for the organization, both in terms of the near future and the far-off horizon. Furthermore, most people expect their executive leaders to be able to move the resources of the organization towards those goals, especially if they are shared with and by the entire team. This is what vision and strategy are all about, the goal, and how to get there.

Setting the Vision

Executive leaders need to be able to not only define a compelling picture of the desired future state of the organization, but also build consensus among the entire organization that the vision is the right one for the entire team. Lots of tools exist to help you define your vision, but here are the steps to take:

  • Look Around: Take stock of where the organization stands today. This is done by conducting a SWOT Analysis (which you should know how to do by now). Consider the external factors shaping the industry and the internal dynamics of the organization. This assessment gives you a baseline of where you are today and where the organization could go from here.
  • Picture the Future: With a clear understanding of the present, imagine the organization at its best, what the real potential of the organization is. What impact can it make? What milestones can be reached? Paint a vivid picture of the desired future state.
  • Craft a Vision Statement: Next you need to distill that vision in your mind into a clear and concise vision statement. The vision statement should be aspirational, inspirational, and easy to understand. It should capture the essence of the desired future state and guide the organization's strategic direction.

Creating the Strategy

Strategy is the high-level plan to achieve the long-term goals of the organization. It considers the competitive landscape, internal capabilities, and market trends. It is NOT a list of tasks; instead it is a guiding document that helps leaders on the team figure out what direction to take things in their areas of responsibility, and high-level targets to aim at for the entire organization.

  • Strategic decision: whether to enter a new market or not
  • Tactical decision: running a specific marketing campaign
  • Key concepts: competitive advantage, differentiation, value proposition, strategic positioning

Here are the keys to thinking strategically:

  • Analyzing industry trends
  • Identifying opportunities and threats
  • Understanding the competitive dynamics between your organization and competitors
  • Understanding the (realistic) internal capabilities of your organization
  • Being aware of the market trends that could change over the coming years

You can use several strategic frameworks to help guide your strategic thinking. There are two videos about Strategic Frameworks that might be valuable for you to go deeper on this topic.

Change Management

Understanding how to change things in large organizations is a key skill that all executives need to learn and master. Let's talk about why change management is needed, and how it works.

The Need for Change Management

Change is constant. Technological, economic, and social changes happen at breakneck speed. Just ask Kodak, Xerox, and Sears about the need to keep up with changing environments.

But change is hard. Very, very hard. The larger the organization, the less likely a change will be effective. Worse, in organizations where the culture is not innovative, the change failure rate can exceed 70%. Oftentimes, change or transformation activities fail in organizations due to a lack of clear communication, resistance to change among the workforce, and inadequate planning / time for adoption. Therefore, it's important to manage the changes that are being implemented.

Key Principles of Change Management

  • Communication: Open, honest and frequent communication. You need to explain the 'why' behind the change you are trying to make.
  • Stakeholder Engagement: You need to involve ALL of the key stakeholders in the process. This should include everyone who is impacted by the change, not just the tech team. Include employees, managers, customers, and vendors if needed, everyone should have a voice if they will be impacted.
  • Leadership Team Champion: Visible and consistent support from the leadership team is important to make sure everyone stays aligned on the change. Without a champion who can drive everyone to do the things needed for the change, the entire thing can fall apart if just left to the program managers and implementation specialists.
  • Training & Support: You need to provide adequate training and resources to help people adapt to the change and adopt the new systems, tools, processes, etc. Without an abundance of training and opportunity to get hands-on with the change, adoption will flag and people will work around the change.

Change Models

Lewin's Change Management Model

This is as simple as change management models get, which is why I LOVE it. The three stages: Unfreeze, Change, Refreeze.

  • Unfreeze: This is where you create awareness and urgency around an upcoming necessary change by explaining how the status quo isn't really viable anymore.
  • Change: This is a time of uncertainty and disruption. Important to communicate clearly and frequently, addressing concerns along the way. Transparency is absolutely critical.
  • Refreeze: This is about solidifying the change and making it the new normal. Reinforce new behaviors (mostly through rewards not punishments), update policies, processes, and systems, and build momentum by acknowledging success.

Kotter's 8-Step Process

This is a structured, step-by-step approach to change, which can be helpful for complex initiatives. It emphasizes creating momentum and buy-in, crucial aspects of change management.

  1. Create a sense of urgency, help people understand why change is needed NOW
  2. Build a guiding coalition, a team of people who understand the change and can inspire others to help
  3. Form a strategic vision, a clear, concise vision of the future state and how to get there
  4. Enlist a volunteer army, this is about creating a real movement in the org
  5. Enable action by removing barriers, find and fix obstacles that prevent people from adopting the new vision
  6. Generate short-term wins, celebrate early successes to build momentum and demonstrate progress
  7. Sustain acceleration, continue to push for change long after the noise dies down
  8. Institute change, embed the change into the culture and processes as the new normal

Other Frameworks

  • McKinsey 7-S Framework: test the organization's readiness for change by measuring interconnected elements, Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Style, Staff.
  • ADKAR Model: good for people-side transitions, Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement.

Organizational Design

What is Organizational Design

This is the process of structuring an organization to effectively achieve its strategic goals. It's about aligning the structure, processes, people, and culture in a way that leads to the best outcomes for everyone, shareholders, employees, customers, etc.

There is a rainbow of organizational designs. Some organizations like to be very flat, with one leader and lots of peers at the same level. Other organizations do better with large hierarchies. Some are matrixed, hybrid, etc. You can also design organizations based on their geography, the lines of products, the industries each division serves, etc. As an executive you will be expected to understand not only how to read complex org charts, but how to build them effectively.

Common Organizational Structures

  • Functional: grouped by expertise / skill (e.g. marketing, finance, operations, etc.)
  • Divisional: grouped by product, geography, or customer segment
  • Matrix: employees report to multiple managers (e.g. a project manager and a functional manager)

Key Org Design Principles

  • Alignment with Strategy: The most important thing to consider when designing your organization is why you are designing it that way. Which structure makes the most sense for what you are trying to do? Take your time to think about the structure not based on the people around you but based on how it should work long after all of you are gone.
  • Clarity of Roles & Responsibilities: Minimize any ambiguity and overlap in your organization. I hate matrix organizations for this reason, the line of leadership should be clear to each individual in the organization, and each individual should have one direct manager.
  • Efficiency & Effectiveness: Good organizations tend to push decision-making down to the lowest level possible, effectively distributing power equally throughout the entire organization. If all of the decision-making is centralized at the top, then why even have any managers or directors?
  • Adaptability: Design your organization for flexibility and responsiveness to change. Be willing to make changes when the organization, or the environment, evolves.
