Momentum & Timing

Time offers so you hold multiple at once, the highest-leverage position in any negotiation.

What you'll learn: the importance of momentum in the executive job hunt, why two (or more) offers is always better than one, and how to time offers to come in at the same time.

Momentum

Momentum is CRUCIAL to the success or failure of your job search. Imagine this scenario: you just got off the phone with the head of HR at "THE" company, the one you've wanted to work for as an executive leader for a long time. She tells you that you are the top candidate by a mile, and that things are going smoothly on the inside.

Now imagine three weeks go by, and that head of HR calls you back to let you know that there has been a change in leadership direction, and they are "re-evaluating the role" and if it fits with the new philosophy. She'll update you in a few months when they have decided if they are going to fill the role. Depending on how you handled this situation, you are either super-stressed, or slightly bummed.

If you started coasting because this job was "THE ONE", now you have to build up all of that momentum again and restart your search from scratch because it's been 3 weeks since you last spoke to the other companies, and you have few to no new leads. If you had kept up your momentum with other opportunities, kept your posting and engagement rates on LinkedIn high, and continued to treat networking and outreach as your primary job, you'd still be disappointed, but not in a bad position.

Do not stop your efforts to find your next role until you've signed the offer sheet.

Having 3-4 offer letters is a good problem. It's a problem sure because you'll have to let some people down, but that's a better problem than the alternative. So don't slow down no matter how comfortable you feel with one particular offer. Until they give you a fully executed copy of the job offer and you have a start date, don't slow down on continuing the search process.

Your goal: multiple offers

Reasoning

Imagine you just landed the job of your dreams. You get the title, the responsibility, the commute, and the team you always wanted, but the compensation was just a tad lighter than you had hoped. You ask about making a change, and they move slightly, but not as much as you had hoped. You have no other recourse so you sign and join the company. This is a good outcome, but it's not a great outcome.

A great outcome would look like this: you just landed the job of your dreams. You get the title, the responsibility, the commute, and the team you always wanted. Simultaneously you just landed an equally great offer letter. It's not the dream role but the compensation and benefits are fantastic. Now you can go back to your dream company and let them know you have a competing offer with compensation that you feel more fairly suits your status in the industry. You have the receipts to back up your request for a change in compensation. It is far more likely they will move to match your competing offer in this scenario. And if they don't, well you have two great options to choose from.

Slowing down offers

In the event you know that you will have an offer coming in the near future, but you are still interviewing with other companies that are very interesting to you, you might want to think about ways to slow down the offer letter (without stopping it) from the first company.

  • You can do this by asking a lot of questions that take time for them to answer.
  • Once you have the offer, you can ask for some time to review it with your lawyer and life partner or others.
  • You can counter the offer with another suggested offer and give them time to respond.
  • Weekends can be your friend here.

Speeding up offers

On the other side of this coin, as soon as you have an offer letter (or know that you are going to get one) you can reach out to the executive recruiter and let them know that you have a competitive offer in your hands (or almost in your hands).

  • They will likely speed up the process internally to determine quickly if you are their top candidate.
  • If you are, they will also likely speed up the offer letter process.
  • If you are not their top candidate, they may push back and tell you the process is what it is.
  • If you can get them to make a formal offer to you within 3-5 business days of the first offer, you'll have the dream scenario of multiple offers to compare simultaneously.

Comparing multiple offers

When you do find yourself in that great situation, where you have multiple job offers sitting on your table in front of you, it's important to make sure you make the best decision possible for you based on what's important to you.

What matters most to you

More important than the actual cash remuneration is how you feel about the work you will be doing. Ask yourself some of these questions:

  • Do you love what the company stands for?
  • Will you have the autonomy or structure that you crave?
  • Do you like the leadership team (and specifically your direct supervisor)?
  • Are you comfortable with the culture of the organization?
  • Are you comfortable with the role and the responsibilities that you will take on?
  • Do you like your team and can you work well with them?
  • Is there significant personal growth opportunity for you in this organization?
  • Will you have the work/life balance that you desire?
  • Are the benefits good? (Speaking from experience, don't skimp on your health insurance or disability coverage, ever.)

The most important question to determine the best offer

Which opportunity sparks a feeling of joy, excitement, enthusiasm, and wonder in you more than the other?

You've already decided

When you're not sure about a decision you need to make, consider this: you've already decided. Here's what I mean. Humans are great at making big decisions quickly. We just suck at realizing we've already decided. We make decisions in a split second because our decision-engine is an emotional one, not a rational one. We can rationalize just about any decision we ever make.

The difficulty in making a decision between two choices is not in which one is the better choice. It's in what will happen if we get the decision wrong. Two things could be very similar in outcome, but one path is always more closely aligned with the head, heart and gut. So you instinctively know which path you want to take, but the fact that you have to decide and the outcome of the other one may be just as great can leave you feeling unsettled about your decision.

The #1 way to make a hard decision

This has worked for me time and again throughout my life. When you are faced with a difficult decision, imagine saying no to one alternative, the one you secretly want. You know which one that is. It's not the logical choice, it's the choice your gut or heart tells you to take. How do you feel in that moment? Really tune in to the feeling of letting go of that opportunity or path. If you are listening, really listening, to your gut, you will either feel anxiety or relief. And then you will have your answer.

From there, the argument in your head is about logic. But remember, decisions are not made based on logic. Logic is used to shore up a decision, but humans make decisions emotionally. Once you understand that, you'll make decisions a lot faster in life.
