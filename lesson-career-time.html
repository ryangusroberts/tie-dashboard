Time Management for Executives

Manage your time, energy, and attention, not just your calendar, so you have both the hours and the capacity to use them.

Nothing feels worse than having time set aside to spend with friends or family, but not having the energy to enjoy that time.

Well, one thing might feel worse, not even having the time to be with family, because you're working so hard, have so much to do, and can't seem to get ahead of your daily life.

So many of us have been there. It's not uncommon to feel like there just isn't enough hours in the day to get everything done. Let's work on that in this training.

The Real Problem: Time, Energy & Attention

Have you noticed that even in the age of AI and automation, very senior executives still have Executive Admins or Administrative Assistants? It's not because it's a perk that makes them feel important. It's because, quite honestly, their time is so valuable that not managing it effectively would waste a lot of the organization's value stored in the leader's ability to make effective decisions.

For the rest of us down here on Earth, we need to find ways to mirror what the most important leaders get from their time being managed for them, building systems that lead to the most effective and efficient use of our time. Not only our time though, but also our Energy and Attention.

You can allocate time from 2-3AM to read a business book, but what good is that allocation if you don't have the energy or the ability to pay attention during that time?

Becoming an Executive means understanding that you can't just throw more hours of your time, energy, and attention at every problem. Given that we only get 24 hours a day, it's not about working 20 and sleeping for four. Instead, it's about prioritizing your time, your energy levels, and your attention span, focusing on the most important and impactful things you can accomplish to take effective actions towards your goals.

The Frame Calendar

The first thing that I find helpful is building what I call a "Frame Calendar." This is a calendar that basically maps out your days, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. It's the default calendar assuming no special events, no changes to what you do.

What you'll notice right away is that every hour of every day is blocked out for something. You'll also notice that the blocks are fairly high-level, such as "Task Work" and "Deep Work." I organize those times on a weekly basis, but I know when during the day to focus on each. I am far more creative in the morning, so I leave that time to build courses like this. I'm better able to focus on people in calls in the afternoon, so I bunch all of my calls into an afternoon window.

Exercise: Building Your Frame Calendar

Let's get to work building your frame calendar. Follow the guidance in this checklist to organize your entire week, 24 hours a day.

Building Your Ideal Schedule:

  • Create a new calendar: Open a new calendar on your computer, or print out a weekly calendar with 24 hours. Your goal is to assign every single hour of your week, all 168 of them.
  • Get granular: Expand each category, creating a list of all the activities you want to include.
  • Realistic time estimates: Don't overcommit. Be honest about how long tasks and routines take.
  • Prime time: Experiment to discover when your energy levels are highest. Block high-focus tasks during that time of day.
  • Color-coding: Color-code different categories on your calendar (blue for self-care, red for family time, etc.) for easy visualization.

Additional guidance: Your calendar doesn't have to be perfect. Don't stress over following it rigidly. It's meant to be a guide that brings you back to something that matches your highest, best use of your time, energy, and attention. I'm not perfect at following this, but it does help me fix my actual calendar when things get a little out of whack. You can be as granular or high-level as you like. I prefer to be high-level so I have flexibility in what I do during those time blocks, and when I actually do it.

Finally, I recommend getting to bed at the same time every day, and waking up the same time every day, including weekends. Your circadian rhythm doesn't understand the concept of the 5-day work week. Every time you adjust your sleep by an hour, it takes a day to reset. So if you change your sleep by 4 hours, it'll take 4 days to recover to the highest quality recovery.

The Theory of Constraints

One of my favorite theories is the Theory of Constraints, developed in the 1980s by Dr. Eliyahu Goldratt. He built it to help factories become more effective, but it applies to all systems in life and business.

Goldratt wrote a book about the theory, called The Goal. It's a part fiction, part non-fiction book that's an easy read. It's very dated (from the 1980s) but does a great job of explaining the theory with practical examples in both factories and business systems, so it's a fast read.

Explaining the theory

The theory basically suggests that you need to find the biggest bottleneck in the system and fix that first. Then, once you've fixed that bottleneck, you look for the next biggest bottleneck and fix that next. And so on.

The thing is, as you fix bottlenecks, you'll create new ones, so the list of bottlenecks is constantly changing. It can feel frustrating going through this process, like playing whack-a-mole with problems, but when you look at things a few weeks or months later, you'll realize just how far you've come in improving your systems.

This works basically like this:

  1. Identify a bottleneck
  2. Fix the bottleneck
  3. Delegate, automate, or eliminate the bottleneck fix
  4. Watch to see if the fix works
  5. Run it back, find the next biggest bottleneck

How to apply the Theory of Constraints to your systems

Take a look at what your biggest challenges are, and what you can do to address those challenges. Let's go through an exercise together to do this.
