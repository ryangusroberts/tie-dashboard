While LinkedIn is 90% of the work in getting a recruiter or hiring manager to consider you at all for an opportunity, the resume is the last 10% that, while not as impactful, is still critically important not to get wrong. Imagine going to a restaurant and having a 5-star meal only to be served Skittles in a napkin for dessert. So let's dive into how you should manage your resume from now on in your executive career.

Six key elements of an executive resume

Let's talk about content over form first. When it comes to assembling your resume, there are six elements that make up a great executive version.

1. Executive Summary

This is the most crucial section of your executive resume. It's the first (and usually ONLY) part a recruiter reads in detail. It should be a concise, powerful narrative (3-5 sentences) with some bullet points that gives a high-level understanding of your value proposition. What to include:

  • Your current or target executive title
  • Your core expertise (e.g., "Digital Transformation Leader," "P&L Executive," "Strategic M&A Specialist")
  • Quantifiable career highlights: ~2-4 of your most impressive, impactful achievements
  • What you bring to a new organization (e.g., "drives market share growth," "optimizes operational efficiency," "builds high-performance cultures")

2. Core Competencies / Key Skills

In this section, include 2-3 columns of keywords that highlight your executive-level skills. This makes it easy for both human resources and Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) to identify your expertise. Some examples: Strategic Planning, Global Operations, Change Management, AI Strategy, Financial Acumen, Board Governance, Talent Acquisition, Cybersecurity Oversight, M&A Experience, Public Speaking, Sales & Business Development (include a $ amount you've closed).

3. Professional Experience

This section is meant to be a narrative on your impact to previous (and future) organizations. For each role you include, detail 5-8 quantifiable achievements that demonstrate your executive impact. Use the CAR (Challenge, Action, Result) and STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) methods:

  • Challenge: What problem or opportunity existed? (e.g., "Legacy systems causing inefficiencies...")
  • Action: What specific, executive-level actions have you taken? (e.g., "Led a cross-functional team to overhaul IT infrastructure...")
  • Result: What was the measurable outcome? (e.g., "...resulting in a 20% reduction in operational costs and a 15% increase in system uptime.")

Quantify everything: numbers are king at the executive level. Use percentages, dollar figures, team sizes, project scopes, timeframes, etc. (e.g., "Managed a $50M P&L," "Grew revenue by 30% over two years," "Led a team of 100+ employees," "Successfully launched 3 new product lines, generating $15M in first-year sales.") Emphasize leadership and strategic impact: how you led teams, drove strategic initiatives, influenced stakeholders, and contributed to overarching goals. Use reverse chronological order (most recent and relevant first). For older roles (10-15+ years back), focus on 1-2 impactful achievements rather than a lengthy list. Consider omitting jobs older than 15 years unless critically relevant to the target role.

4. Education & Certifications

Brevity: for executives, this section is typically concise. List degrees (highest first), institution names, and locations. Graduation dates should often be omitted to prevent age bias. Highlight relevant executive programs, leadership certifications, or specialized industry credentials.

5. Optional sections (adding value strategically)

You can add 1-2 additional sections if it's additive to your narrative. Remember we're not bragging here, we're giving someone as much of a 3D view of you in a 2D format.

  • Board memberships: if applicable, include non-profit or corporate board roles.
  • Professional affiliations/associations: mention industry leadership roles or significant memberships.
  • Publications/speaking engagements: demonstrate your thought leadership abilities.
  • Awards & recognition: industry awards, company accolades.
  • Community involvement: keep it brief and relevant to leadership and your values.

6. Overall design & formatting (professional & ATS-friendly)

Use a clean, traditional layout with ample white space. Avoid fancy graphics, photos, or complex multi-column layouts that can confuse the ATS. Use professional, legible fonts, preferably sans serif (Calibri, Arial, Aptos), with appropriate font sizes (11-12 for the body, up to 20+ for name/headings). Executive resumes can be 2-3 pages, but try to stick to 2 (front and back of one piece of paper). 1 is too short, 3 is too long, 2 feels like the sweet spot. ALWAYS send as a PDF to preserve the formatting unless the organization specifically requests a different format.

The VM Complete Resume System

Now that you have a sense of what a good resume looks like, let's build your complete resume system that will make it super-easy (and fast) to create custom-tailored resumes on the fly. It can be daunting to rebuild your resume over and over to apply for specific jobs, but it's worth the effort. Remember you are the CEO of You, Inc., and a hyper-tailored resume is like sending out a heat-seeking missile vs. carpet-bombing and hoping for the best.

1. The Core Resume Document

This is your evergreen, comprehensive, highly-detailed living document. It contains every significant accomplishment, project, responsibility, skill, and experience in your executive career. It's not uncommon for this document to exceed 10 pages, which is fine. It's your "boilerplate" from which you build specific resumes. No page limit, include all potential keywords, metrics, and detailed CAR/STAR stories. Update it regularly (quarterly, or after major projects) to capture successes while they are fresh. A Word document or similar editable format will suffice (I like Notion, so I keep mine there).

2. The Target-Specific Resume Templates

From the core resume, create 2-3 core "templates" based on typical executive roles or industries you might target (e.g., "COO Resume," "Strategy Leader Resume," "Digital Transformation Executive Resume"). Each template starts with a pre-selected set of relevant achievements, skills, and a summary tailored to that type of role. When a specific job application comes up, start with the most relevant template, then fine-tune it for that role.

3. The Tailoring Toolkit

  • Job description deconstruction: meticulously analyze job descriptions for keywords, required skills, and core challenges, and tailor your resume to their language as much as possible.
  • Keyword bank: maintain a running list of keywords extracted from appealing job descriptions.
  • "Brag book" / accomplishment journal: keep a digital or physical journal where you continuously log your achievements, results, and specific actions taken. This feeds directly into the core resume.
  • AI for initial drafts/brainstorming (with heavy human oversight): AI can help brainstorm achievement statements or summarize sections. Always remember the final output must be personalized, quantified, and reflect your unique executive voice.

4. The Proofreading & Feedback Loop (with help from AI)

  • Self-proofreading: crucial. Read it aloud, check for consistency, and use grammar checkers.
  • AI review: despite the guidance to never submit AI-generated content as your own, you can and should have an AI LLM (Gemini, GPT, etc.) review your resume, especially in comparison to the role. Give the AI both the resume and the position description, and see how it considers your chances to get an interview.
  • External review: have a trusted peer, mentor, or professional review the resume for clarity, impact, and errors. A fresh pair of eyes often catches what the author misses.

Some final resume tips

How recruiters assess your resume

  • Do you have the core tech skills to do the job?
  • Do your titles match?
  • Do you have relevant experience?
  • Do you have enough years of experience?
  • Is your industry relevant?
  • Are you in the geographic area or do you require relocation?
  • Do you have career stability, or do you lack direction?

Ineffective resumes

  • Hard to find contact information
  • Outdated email addresses, fonts and layouts
  • No dates of employment or not in chronological order
  • Tasks/responsibilities vs. accomplishments
  • Outdated skills or tools
  • Cutting & pasting job descriptions
  • Too wordy (3+ line bullet points, 3 pages)

Combatting ageism

Candidates over the age of 40 should:

  • Consider consolidating early career roles under "other employment" or "other relevant experience."
  • Demonstrate your experience with modern skills and accomplishments.
  • Remove dates from education.
  • Be mindful of outdated technology, job titles, and email addresses such as AOL, Hotmail or Yahoo. Gmail is preferred.
