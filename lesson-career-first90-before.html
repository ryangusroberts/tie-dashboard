Before The First Day

Set yourself up before you start: the pre-start checklist, the Influencer Diagram, and the breakeven mindset.

What you'll learn: what to do before your start date, the Influencer Diagram, and the breakeven mindset for your first 90 days.

This whole period of time between signing the offer letter and start date is a great opportunity to get ahead of the curve on the absolute fire hose that's about to get turned on you. Get as many things aligned and organized and agreed to as you can during this time, so when the chaos starts you've at least mastered the basics.

Day -30 to Day -1

From the moment you sign the offer letter, you are effectively 'on the clock' as a new executive in your new company. Obviously you are not yet on the payroll, but there are some things you should be doing to prepare yourself for your new role. This is what to do from Day -30 to Day 0.

Take a mental break

I have rolled from one job right into another multiple times in my career with little to no break in-between. But I have found that taking a few weeks (sometimes as much as a month) in between jobs helps you to rest your mind, refresh yourself, and prepare mentally for an entirely new marathon. I STRONGLY encourage you to take at least two weeks off after off-boarding from your previous role before starting your new role, and I would highly recommend taking at least a month off if you can make it work. This time will help you tremendously by resetting your mindset and putting some separation between the two roles. It is so rare to have a moment where you are not working in support of an organization's goals. The space in between jobs can be an incredibly recharging space if you let it be.

A clean start

Getting started in a new role can be exciting, but you really want to try to put some time and space between your acceptance letter and your start date to take care of several things. Let's go through that list now.

Wrapping up your previous role

The first and foremost thing to do once you've accepted and received a countersigned employment offer is to resign from your previous role. Refer to the Interviewing & Negotiation module for guidance on how to do this appropriately. Most companies will expect you to offer 2-3 weeks of time to off-board. The more valuable and critical you are to the company's success, the more time they may request of you. At the senior executive level, it's not uncommon for this process to take months. So make sure you leave enough time between your offer acceptance and start date to clean everything up at your current position.

Cleaning up digitally

This is a good time to assess your digital hygiene. You might have a bunch of work product or files / templates that partially belong to you and/or your former employer on your personal computer, or you might have a mess of contacts that are in multiple contact lists. Anything that could be considered proprietary to your company you should copy up to their servers (or on to your work laptop, however that works in your organization) and remove from your own personal electronics.

The one caveat to that is your contact list. Specifically your contact list in your company smartphone. That list is something you can take with you. Your personal and professional relationships are not proprietary to the company at which you created those relationships. What you can NOT take with you is detailed customer information that would be found, say, in a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) database. So make sure you port your contacts from your work account over to your personal account (Gmail, iCloud, etc.) before you hand back your smartphone.

Research & study

During your break, you want to learn about the new company's history, mission, vision, values, culture, etc. You should have done a good bit of research on this company as part of the interview process, but now is a great time to dive in even further. This is also a good time to familiarize yourself with the company's competitors, industry, regulators, etc. You want to understand the market you're entering. Find some of the industry trade magazines and blogs and subscribe to them. You can also sometimes get your hands on industry reports from tradeshows and conferences.

If the company is public, I suggest reviewing their Annual Report, financial statements, and any recent press releases. Most public companies have an Investor Center on their website, but all annual reports can be found on the SEC website (sec.gov/search-filings).

HR benefits & withholdings

Make sure all of your HR paperwork is complete. As someone who used to just check boxes without paying any attention to HR forms, I now highly recommend understanding the details of your benefits packages, your contracts with the employer (non-disclosures, e.g.), and all of those kinds of things. This is also a good time to speak with your financial advisor to make sure you're taking home the right amount of income, investing the right amount, and setting aside enough for your tax bill.

Reach out early & often

Before you start, you should be talking regularly with your soon-to-be direct supervisor. Your goal here is to make sure that you and s/he agree on what success looks like for you in your new role, and that you know what you are about to jump into. Ask your direct supervisor about their ideas, suggestions, and concerns. Whatever their biggest concerns and challenges are, those are now your biggest concerns and challenges.

Draft a 90-day agenda

If your supervisor hasn't already given you one, you might want to build a 90-day agenda for yourself. This is a simple 1-page document that briefly summarizes what you'll be doing in the first 3 months on the job. Outline your key priorities, focus your efforts on the most important issues, and think of it like a to-do list.

Prepare for your teammates

Learn as much as you can not only about the company, but also about the people inside the company you'll work with on a daily basis. Who will be reporting to you? What are they like? What do they expect of you? Who will you report to besides your direct supervisor? What competing needs do they have that might be in conflict with your direct's needs?

Logistical preparation

Will you be an on-site employee? If so, what effects will you bring to the office? What equipment will you need? What is the commute like? Parking? Nearby restaurants, gyms, coffee shops, co-working spaces, etc. If you're going to be remote, what can you do to prepare your home office space for the new role? Now is a good time to think about upgrading your webcam, microphone and lighting to provide a more professional appearance online. And if you're in a hybrid role or a high-travel role, what preparations do you need to make in order to be able to book travel regularly?

The Influencer Diagram

When I was in Business Development, I learned how to map an organization not by the org chart but by the key decision makers and the people who influenced them. I called it the Influencer Diagram. It was one of the greatest tools I had available to know where, when, and how to influence an organization to work with my company. It worked pretty well, I closed more than $1.5B in business using this tool in my career. Turns out you can adapt this tool to your work environment in order to understand who has the key decision-making authority when it comes to your career and growth inside an organization.

Map everyone who works with you, color-code the relationship (green/yellow/white/red), tag their role (KDM, A for approver, INF for influencer), and rate their influence 1-5.

Fill in the influencer chart

  • Define the players: create a box for everyone who works with you. Lay this out hierarchically, as in peers on your level, your supervisor and his/her peers one layer up, etc. Organize them left to right in terms of how close to you they are in the org chart, or how often you interact with them.
  • Color the relationship: try to determine the relationship you have with all of these folks. Green means great. Yellow means neutral relationship. White means little to no relationship, and Red is bad.
  • Determine their role in decision-making regarding your role. Your direct supervisor will most often be the Key Decision Maker (KDM) for your role, but not always. And there may be multiple KDMs depending on how matrixed you are. A is for approvers (people who sign off on your decisions when lots of money is involved) and INF is for people who influence these decisions.
  • Rate their level of influence. Someone with a 5 influence has a very high level of influence on whether you can or can't do something, whereas someone with a 1 has some influence but very little.

Recommended reading: the first 90 days

Along with this portion of the program you should be reading The First 90 Days by Michael Watkins. The best takeaway from this book really is that you need time to return the value they are investing in you, so take your time making decisions, and don't worry about demonstrating your value right away.

You will consume a ton of value from the organization at first, and somewhere around month 3 you will start to contribute more value than you consume. Around month 6 you'll have contributed equal value that you've consumed, so from that point forward you should be delivering more value than you consume continuously. This is the breakeven point.

You consume more value than you contribute at first. Around month 6 the lines cross; from there you deliver more than you consume.

The book lays out a set of proven practices for new-job success. As you work through this module, use it alongside these lessons to check where you are in applying those practices for effectiveness on the job.
