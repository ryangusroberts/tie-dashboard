The goal of this training is to learn and practice writing, talking, presenting, and generally communicating in your newly-found executive presence and style.

Understanding how executives communicate involves more than just verbal communication. Often executives are communicating more by listening than speaking. Sometimes, replying all on that email is the wrong way to go. And sometimes you need to set a meeting up for success before it turns into a train wreck. Let's dive in.

Executive Communications Primer

What makes for really effective executive communications? Think about how you expect a Fortune 100 CEO to speak when they are a guest on CNBC, or the leader of a large organization addresses a room of 1,000 guests at a keynote for an industry trade show. Do you communicate the same way as them? And what is it about their communications that are different than the way managers and individual contributors communicate?

Definition

Executive Communication refers to the strategic and effective exchange of information, ideas, and messages by senior leaders within an organization. It encompasses the comprehensive set of skills, strategies, and practices that executives employ to communicate with clarity, impact, and influence.

At its core, executive communication focuses on conveying the organization's vision, strategies, and goals to various internal and external stakeholders. It involves articulating the overarching strategic direction, inspiring and motivating employees, building relationships with stakeholders, and representing the organization's interests to the wider public.

Executive communication encompasses both verbal and non-verbal communication methods. It includes delivering compelling presentations, engaging in strategic conversations, composing written materials, and utilizing various communication channels such as meetings, emails, town hall sessions, and social media platforms.

Effective executive communication entails adapting messages to diverse audiences, considering cultural nuances, and ensuring clarity and consistency in the delivery of information. It involves active listening, empathy, and the ability to influence and persuade others towards desired outcomes.

What Makes For Good Executive Communication

  • Great executives are clear and concise in their communication. If you want to amplify a message, simplify it.
  • Great executives want to hear, and will present, the Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF). Don't waste an executive's time telling a story before you have shared the landing point.
  • Great executives tend to say less, which tends to say more.
  • Great executives ask more questions than they give declaratives. This is because executives aren't supposed to run the operations of the company. They are supposed to build the team and the systems that run the operations of the company. In a highly effective organization, the directors and managers answer the questions the executives ask, and then make the declarations of what to do.
  • Great executives also communicate more than decisions. They communicate culture, approval or disapproval, vision, strategy, and expectations for success.

Methods of Executive Communication

Email

With every single email you send, ask yourself "Should I send this email?" Email should be your LAST form of communication, not your first.

  • Emails from executives carry a LOT of weight, they become de-facto doctrine for the team reading them
  • The tone of email matters, and is often misconstrued by the reader
  • It is ALWAYS best to have a face-to-face conversation. If you can't, then have a video call. If you can't, then have a phone call. If you can't, then leave a voice message. If you can't, then text with context and emojis. If you can't, then send an email but offer to follow up for more context.
  • Emails are good for announcements and documenting some significant change
  • NEVER send an emotional response by email
  • It's always better to win the long-term battle than to be right in that moment. Lose the battle but go win the war.
  • Hover but don't press send
Pro tip: If you HAVE to respond, write your response with nobody in the To: line, then go for a 15-minute walk, then delete it. Always better to answer an emotional email with a face-to-face or a call.

Presentations

Your presentations to other executives are too long, too full of jargon/acronyms, and in the wrong order.

Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF). Always present the recommended Course of Action (COA) first, then supporting information later. Executives want the COA and then may poke at your reasoning/support to make sure you've done the homework. As they build trust in you, you won't need to present backup data.

The Pyramid Principle. Watch the video on how consultants build decks, and how you should too.

Don't Rely on PowerPoints. The best presentation is given WITHOUT a PowerPoint. Slides are supposed to support a presenter, not BE the presenter. The best presentation I ever saw had 1 slide with 6 words on it for 55 minutes.

Not All Decks are Presentations. There are multiple types of slide decks / PowerPoints that are NOT presentations: read-ahead decks (that you email in lieu of presenting in person), business plan decks (like a Word document but landscape and prettier), and technical / financial / operational reports.

Other advice on presentations:

  • NEVER READ YOUR SLIDES. I can read them. And if you're reading slides to me, I am wondering to myself why this isn't an email.
  • NEVER use audio or video, especially if you are presenting remotely
  • Consider the 10-20-30 rule: 10 slides, 20 minutes, 30-point font
  • The fewer words the better
  • Don't present after lunch, we are all too tired to pay attention to you

Communicating with Subordinates (Down)

  • Your team looks to you for guidance, protection, and their own growth
  • Help them grow individually and as a team
  • The more transparent you are, the more they will trust you and be transparent with you
  • Clear, concise, honest, direct, transparent, consistent

Use the Manager Tools Trinity of Management Techniques (see Executive Management training before this one for more):

  • Feedback, 90% positive, 10% constructive, give feedback daily so it's not a surprise
  • Coaching, constantly teach your team members skills to grow as leaders themselves. Great leaders produce great leaders.
  • Delegation, big ball concept
  • One-on-One Meetings, meet weekly for 30 minutes with each subordinate: 10 minutes for them to share anything with you, 10 minutes for what you need from them, 10 minutes to talk about the future (theirs, yours, the company)

Communicating with Peers (Parallel)

  • Always be respectful to your peers. Any one of them could be your boss at any time.
  • Build solid relationships (alliances) to help advance your goals (and help them advance theirs).
  • Overshare your goals and objectives with your peers.
  • Listen and help, they will be your network in the future.
  • Offer to help them advance in their career, especially if you are on separate paths in the same organization.
  • Trust, but not too much. Sometimes a peer will only see you as a threat to their career. They might work to undermine your own growth. This is unfortunate but happens more and more the higher you go.

Communicating Up (to Senior Leadership or Board)

  • Be brief and be clear, the greatest gift to executives is time
  • Always bring 1-3 solutions with every problem you bring to them
  • Optimism and honesty always works best, even if they don't like it
  • Don't work with a leader who doesn't appreciate honesty
  • Be very clear about your goals and ambitions, there's no shame in wanting to grow
  • Ask for feedback and coaching constantly
  • If they don't already, ask for weekly one-on-one meetings
  • Always tie your concepts back to the corporate vision

Five Rules for Communicating with an Executive Team

  • Escape the minutiae (the details, that's for doers/implementers)
  • Exude unshakeable confidence, do not act as if you are inferior. They are people just like you, so act normal around them, the way you would your peers.
  • Execute Rainmaking Communications, always discuss thoughts in context of benefit to people, planet, or profit
  • Elongate your timeframes (think in terms of years, decades, not weeks/months, this helps you get less bothered about drama in the ranks and focused upward/outward)
  • Think Big Picture, what is the next best thing for this organization?

Meetings

Meetings can be the most effective way to communicate and problem solve. Meetings can also be the most effective way to anger, bore, and waste the time of everyone involved. And they're usually the latter.

Consider this: how much money does a meeting of the entire C-suite listening to a 60-minute meandering PowerPoint (that could have been an email) being read to them cost?

  • No meeting should be 1 hour long. 20 minutes or 2 hours.
  • Decision meeting; Brainstorming / working session meeting
  • Book a 30-minute or 2-hour meeting, then give 10 minutes back at the end of each meeting
  • Meetings should have clear agendas AND a moderator/leader. Meeting creator sets the agenda.
  • Make it clear what the goal / outcome of the meeting is
  • Send presentation materials with the invite. If this is uncomfortable, your meeting should be an email.
  • One person should be leading the meeting
  • Define for your staff what you expect for meeting invites
  • Decline meetings that don't follow your guidance on how meetings should be conducted
  • Routinely review recurring meetings that just 'exist' on your calendar
  • Regularly remind your team of your meeting expectations
  • Never let a meeting go off track. If you're the organizer/moderator, remind the offending person what we're here to do. If a fight breaks out, offer to table the topic for later (or get the popcorn).
Your time as an executive is the company's most precious resource. Do not waste it.

Remote Worker Communications

Here is where the meeting rules can change a bit. We run a 100% remote team here at Vertical Motion and have a great working rapport with one another, but it takes effort.

  • Need face-to-face time with remotes, but Zoom fatigue is a thing
  • Work with each individual on what works best for them (some like Zoom calls all day, some hate Zoom calls period)
  • Regularly scheduled working sessions help integrate mixed local/remote teams
  • Daily check-ins on progress are helpful with remote employees
  • Remote workers tend to fall behind on-site peers in social and personal development. But not always, every individual is different.
  • Bring everyone together in person at least twice a year, more if possible.
