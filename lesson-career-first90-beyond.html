Beyond The First 90 Days

Long-term success: annual plans, KPIs/OKRs/SOPs, and never abandoning You, Inc.

Once you've completed your First 90 Days as an executive, it's a good time to take a moment and get very strategic about what you can do to improve the organization from your current role, as well as consider where you want your career to go from here.

Building annual plans

One of the most important responsibilities of true executives is to craft a strategic plan for their organization that is in alignment with the goals of the C-suite and Board. You will most likely be involved in the annual planning process of your organization, and this is where you finally get to flex that strategic muscle you've been exercising for so long.

First things first: get the full picture

Before you even start sketching out ideas, you need to know what the company's all about.

  • Know the company's big goals: every company has its main mission, its big dream, and its top goals for the year. Your plan needs to fit right into that. If the company already has its yearly plan, your job is to make sure your team's work helps hit those overall company targets.
  • Use what you learned: remember all those conversations and digging you did in your first 90 days? Use all that information! What are the biggest problems? Where are the best chances to grow? Your plan should tackle these head-on.
  • What does your boss want? Sit down with your CEO or the person who hired you. What are the 1-3 must-do things they expect from your department this year? Get crystal clear on this.

Pick your battles: focus on key areas

As an executive, you can't do everything. Your job is to figure out the few most important things that will make the biggest difference.

  • Choose 3-5 main goals: what are the absolute biggest things your team needs to achieve this year? These should be things that really push the company forward and solve major problems or grab big opportunities. Think of them as your "big rocks", the things that have to get done.
  • Set clear targets: for each main goal, set a clear target that you can measure. Don't just say "make customers happier." Say "Boost our customer satisfaction score by 10 points by December." This makes it clear what success looks like.
  • Decide where to put your money and time: this is where you show off your executive smarts. As you pick your main goals, think about where you'll spend your budget, your team's time, and even your own energy. What will you invest in, and what might you need to cut back on?

Get everyone on board: work with your team

You're a leader of a team, not a superstar individual contributor. Building the plan is a team effort.

  • Involve your top people early: the worst thing you could do is draft the entire plan yourself and hand it to your team. Instead, get your direct reports and other key leaders in your department involved from the start. Ask for their ideas, challenge their thinking, and let them take ownership of parts of the plan. This makes them feel invested and ensures they'll help make it happen.
  • Connect with other teams: talk to other executives in different departments. How does your plan help them, and how do their plans affect yours? Look for ways to work together so everyone benefits from the plan being assembled.
  • Get your boss's go-ahead: once you have a strong draft, present it to your CEO or other senior leaders. Be ready to explain why these are your top goals, what kind of results you expect, and how you'll handle any bumps in the road. This is your chance to show you've thought it all through and get their full support.

Make it happen: put your plan into action

A plan is only good if you follow through.

  • Share it clearly: once your plan is approved, make sure your entire team understands it. Every single person should know how their daily work helps achieve the big goals. Keep talking about it and use different ways to share the message.
  • Check your progress regularly: schedule regular check-ins (maybe every three months) to see how things are going. What's working? What's getting in the way? Are there any changes you need to make?
  • Be ready to adjust: while your plan gives you a clear path, be flexible enough to deal with new opportunities or problems that pop up. No plan survives contact with reality, and a plan that can't change quickly becomes useless.

KPIs, OKRs, and SOPs

Now that you've developed your annual plan, how do you make sure your team actually hits those goals and does things consistently and correctly? That's where Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) come in. Think of these as your navigation system and instruction manual for success. Here's how to put them to work.

KPIs and OKRs as success trackers

KPIs and OKRs are how you measure if you're winning.

  • KPIs (Key Performance Indicators): these are like the dashboard lights in your car, they tell you how things are running right now. For an executive, focus on a few big-picture KPIs that show the health of your department and how it's contributing to the company's main goals. Examples: revenue growth, customer retention rate, employee engagement scores, project completion rate. Don't drown yourself in too many numbers; pick the ones that truly show if you're on track.
  • OKRs (Objectives and Key Results): these are about what you want to achieve and how you'll know you achieved it. Remember those 3-5 main goals from your annual plan? Those are your Objectives. For each Objective, set 2-4 Key Results that are clear, measurable, and challenging. If your objective is "Launch a game-changing new product," your Key Results might be "Achieve 10,000 sign-ups in the first month" and "Generate $1M in revenue by Q4."

Make them clear and shared

  • Communicate, communicate, communicate: when you roll out these KPIs and OKRs, explain why they matter and how they connect to the company's bigger picture. Your team needs to see the link between their daily work and these big goals.
  • Get buy-in: it's not just your plan; it's our plan. Work with your team leaders to set their own OKRs that connect to yours. When people help build the targets, they're much more likely to own them.
  • Make them visible (dashboard power): put your key KPIs and OKRs on a dashboard that's easy for everyone to see. This could be a shared screen, a simple report, or a digital tool. Transparency keeps everyone focused and accountable.

Building the "how-to" guides: SOPs

SOPs are your team's instruction manual for doing things the right way, every time.

  • Find your "must-get-right" tasks: what are the tasks or activities in your department that absolutely must be done consistently and correctly to avoid mistakes, ensure quality, or meet rules? These are prime candidates for SOPs. Think about things like onboarding new employees, handling customer complaints, processing orders, or closing out projects.
  • Look for bottlenecks or mistakes: if your team keeps running into the same problems or doing things differently, that's a signal you need an SOP.
  • Don't overcomplicate it: an SOP should be easy to understand, even for someone new. Use bullet points, clear language (like 9th-grade level!), and maybe even flowcharts or pictures. It's not a novel; it's a guide.
  • Involve the people who do the work: the best SOPs are written by or with the input of the people who actually perform the tasks daily. They know the ins and outs better than anyone. This also makes them more likely to use it.
  • Easy to find: store your SOPs in a central, easy-to-access place (like a shared drive or internal wiki). No one will use them if they can't find them.
  • Review and update: business changes, and so should your SOPs. Set a schedule to review them (e.g., once a year) to make sure they're still accurate and effective. If a process changes, update the SOP right away.

Managing for success: using your tools

The beauty of having effective KPIs, OKRs, and SOPs is that there is no ambiguity about whether something is done correctly or not. Use your KPIs and OKRs to drive success on your team.

  • Weekly/bi-weekly KPI check-ins: quickly look at your key metrics. Are they green, yellow, or red? What's going well? What needs attention now?
  • Monthly/quarterly OKR reviews: dig deeper into your OKRs. Are you on track to hit your Key Results? What adjustments are needed? Are there any roadblocks your team needs your help to remove?
  • Track, but also take action: numbers and procedures are useless if you don't do anything with them. When you see a problem (a KPI is red, an OKR is behind), quickly figure out why and then decide what action you or your team needs to take.
  • Acknowledge progress: when your team hits a KPI target or makes progress on an OKR, celebrate it! This builds morale and shows people their hard work is noticed.
  • Use setbacks as learning moments: if you miss a target, don't just blame. Ask "What can we learn from this?" and "How can we do better next time?" This creates a culture of continuous improvement.
  • Lead by example: show your team that you rely on these KPIs, OKRs, and SOPs. Refer to them in meetings, use them to make decisions, and show that they guide your own work. Your team will follow your lead.

Some last-minute advice

What to do if the role isn't right for you

It happens from time to time. Some of our clients have joined companies and taken new roles that seem aligned to their skills and values, but then learn that there is a huge delta between what they signed on for or were told to expect, and what they actually signed up for. Once inside, they sometimes discover all the things that were not mentioned during the interview and investigation process. Things like politics, culture, leadership change, downsizing, etc.

So what do you do? Stay and suffer? Leave without a plan? What is your next move? How do you even survive this? First and foremost, this is why you have to see yourself as a Free Agent in the marketplace. Go where you think you will be successful, and if you get there and it's not what you thought, be ready to fire up the search for a better environment. If this happens to you, reach out to the coaches or the community for advice on how to handle this, but don't feel distraught. It unfortunately happens more often than any of us care to admit. You don't have to suffer in silence. You can change your career quickly if needed.

Remember to stay focused on your brand

We've talked a lot this module about how to really nail the First 90 Days (and beyond) of your new executive role. But it's incredibly important to remember that you need to continue to maintain your cadence of posting content on LinkedIn, reaching out to people in your network, and keeping your "Marketing" department staffed at You, Inc. Just because you are gainfully employed in a role you love doesn't mean this is the end of your executive career journey. In fact, it's literally just begun. Instead, continue to invest into your brand. Attend events, meet people, build connections, share great content, do all the things that got you here continuously so you always have warm connections and opportunities available to you if things change at your current organization.
