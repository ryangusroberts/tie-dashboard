The First 90 Days

A step-by-step plan: observe (1-30), assess (31-60), and implement (61-90).

What you'll learn: Days 1-30 (observe), Days 31-60 (assess), and Days 61-90 (implement).
Resist the urge to fix everything on day one. Observe, then assess, then implement.

Days 1-30: observation

In the first week you want to have had as many conversations with as many influencers and stakeholders as possible. This is doubly important if you are a remote executive and only at Headquarters for the week.

Determine immediate priorities

Once you've had a chance to meet all of the key folks (your team, your leadership, your peers, etc.) you'll want to reconnect with your direct supervisor and make sure that you and s/he are on the same page about what your immediate priorities should be. You may get a rainbow of responses ranging from "do what you think is best" to "do these 3 things right now and come back when you're done" so make sure you are working with a direct supervisor that aligns well with your style. Figure out what pressing issues or 'fires' need to be put out right away. There will likely be some things left undone by your predecessor, or if the role is brand new, it was created for a reason (to address a problem area at the executive level).

Watch and learn

For the rest of the first month, you really want to try to be in observation mode. Your goal is to get familiar with the key processes, the cadence of business and the stakeholders. Certainly make decisions when asked by your leadership team to do so, but try to spend as much time just observing how the organization works without you tinkering with it just yet. This will help you understand what areas to prioritize fixing and improving, and which areas to leave alone.

As an executive your job is to make sure the organization works. Let your managers do the managing, and let your individual contributors do the work. Just because you can do those things better doesn't mean that is your mandate, or your highest, best use in the organization. A great executive will give space to the team to do the work, and only get involved when the machine is broken or producing the wrong kind of output.

Score a quick win

During your first month on the job, you want to look around for something that is super easy for you to fix but has flummoxed the organization for a period of time. This is how you demonstrate the fact that while you are new to the organization you are not new to the industry or the game. I like for this to be something really simple:

  • An introduction to a customer with whom I have a good relationship that has ignored their sales team for a long time.
  • A tool (like a spreadsheet) that I have used elsewhere in my career that can help organize something this company isn't good at organizing.
  • Taking on responsibility to fix a small, one-time issue that has frustrated several people for months.
  • Sharing publicly available information about a customer or competitor that they may not be aware of.

A great example of a quick win comes from one of our clients. He took over a team of 125 contractors working in a Government facility. They had no food, coffee, or snacks for the break room because they weren't included in the contract, so while their Government associates had these perks, these folks had to bring coffee in from Starbucks or a thermos. He got budget approved from his corporate manager and stocked the break room with snacks, drinks, and coffee. The team was greatly appreciative of his efforts and leaned in to helping him succeed as their new supervisor.

Days 31-60: assessing your new organization

After a few weeks you should start to have a feel for things now. You're getting comfortable with the pace and style of communication. You're starting to understand all of the personalities. And you are no doubt beginning to see all of the things that were somewhat hidden during the interviewing period. Just like moving in with a significant other, they can only keep their dirty habits and messes a secret for so long after you're on the inside. Expect that you will find these kinds of things throughout the first 3 months.

Continue to build relationships

After you've conducted your initial listening tour, you want to extend that project to include other departments or teams. You may want to consider attending, or even initiating, team building events or lunches as part of your outreach.

Understanding the true culture

This is also about when you should be getting a grasp for the variance (if any) between the stated vs. lived values of the organization. Anyone can write a nice mission statement or create an acronym of values to put on the wall. It takes a special organization to actually live those stated values. Now that you've had time to see the culture from the inside, how closely aligned are the organization's stated vs. lived values? Try to identify champions of the culture, as well as any potential resistors or detractors that might be ideal candidates to remove from the environment.

Evaluate team dynamics & capabilities

By now you should have a good idea of how each member of your own team performs. Ideally you should have been having weekly 1:1 meetings with them (reference the Executive Management lesson for how to do this) and should be clear on what their goals, ambitions, strengths, weaknesses, and blockers are. Now you want to start thinking about which members of your team are worth investing in, which are not, and which ones really just need to find another home. Take your time with this evaluation. There are a number of tools out there to help you evaluate talent, and almost certainly your HR department will provide you with something. But in the rare case you have no talent assessment tools, I recommend using the 9 Box Grid method.

Professional development

Take some time to identify training or other resources that you or your team will need to be successful going forward. What skills do your team lack that you need them to have? These might be skills that weren't necessary for how things run today, but you may have a vision for how your team might do things differently in the future. Consider asking for executive coaching or mentorship funding for you and/or a handful of your top performers. (For example, Vertical Motion has a mastermind program for executives.)

Strategic planning

During this period of time you also want to begin outlining your vision and strategy for the department or organization. Again there are a number of tools to help you develop this strategy and almost certainly your organization will have some sort of strategic planning process into which you can fit. This is where you can begin to put your stamp on the organization. Be careful not to tinker too much with parts of the organization that work well, even if you don't agree with how these parts work. Take your time to rebuild the engine correctly while mid-flight so you can avoid having to try to restart it on the way down. You want to make sure that you solicit input and feedback on your proposed ideas and plans. Understand that while you are the executive in charge of your team, there is a difference between a wanderer and a leader. A leader takes his/her team with them, whereas a wanderer just goes off in random directions with nobody to follow.

Engage with the larger organization

Now is also a good time to begin an outreach campaign to the larger organization by attending corporate meetings, company-wide all-hands events, and town halls. You can also increase your exposure by joining or initiating cross-departmental projects or committees that aim to build something useful.

Days 61-90: implementing your vision

In the third month of your time in a new executive role, you should be feeling like you have traction with some of your ideas, that the organization is growing accustomed to having you there, and that you are settling into the driver's seat of your portion of the organization.

Implementing your vision

This is why you've done all of this work. You're ready to begin implementing your vision for your team. You can see how some things work perfectly, and how some things are irreparably broken. And you can see all the things in between that are somewhat good and bad. You know how all the systems work together, and have insight on how to tinker with the systems to get the most out of them. Let's start doing the real work of an executive: implementing changes to fine-tune the system.

Small process improvements

Around the beginning of the third month you should be able to start implementing some small process improvements. Start out small so that you can demonstrate some quick wins and make sure the entire team is onboard with the changes you want to make. Make sure that you monitor the effects of your changes and gather feedback on how well they were received by everyone involved.

Launch larger initiatives

Once you have a handful of small process improvements underway and receiving positive feedback, you can begin to launch some larger projects. Start implementing more significant projects or changes inside your organization that you feel will really move the needle on performance inside your part of the organization. When you do launch these initiatives, it's important that you have buy-in from everyone in your Influencer Chart, especially your direct supervisor. I find it is always better to get collaborative buy-in from as many stakeholders as possible vs. surprising people with a change and claiming all the credit. It's far better to share the success of an implementation you conjured up than to outright own the failure of a brilliant plan that nobody else embraced.

Feedback loop

Towards the end of your first 90 days you also want to start to gather feedback from everyone in the organization that has worked with you. Typically this looks like a 360-degree Feedback Assessment. These are questionnaires that are sent to your direct reports, peers, and direct supervisors that work with you on a daily basis. They are asked a number of questions about you and what it's like to work with you. The feedback can help you grow tremendously if you accept the feedback with the intention it was delivered: to improve your leadership ability. I strongly recommend having an outside coach (or HR professional) administrate the 360-Feedback session if and when possible.

Review & adjust goals

You can also go back and review the goals you set with your direct supervisor back on Day 1 of your time in the company, and make adjustments or tweaks to the overall strategy. 90 Days is a great time to realign yourself with the dynamically changing needs of the leadership team. We are constantly finding out new information and seeing changes in the environment, so don't be surprised if your plan on Day 1 looks much different than the plan you rebuild on Day 90.

Plan for the future

Finally, set your sights out farther ahead in the future and look past the next 90 days. This is the time to start setting a vision for the next six months, year, and even two year time periods. You want to start aligning your budget, resources, personnel, and attention towards supporting that larger vision.

Congratulations

And with all of that, I have to congratulate you for making it this far, because at this point, you are now a fully functioning executive in an organization that is undoubtedly grateful to have you on their team.
