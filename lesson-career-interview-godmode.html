Why interviewing causes anxiety

Isn't it fascinating how we get so worked up over a job interview? Where does all that anxiety and stress really come from?

Genetic & tribal anxiety

Turns out it's genetically wired into our DNA. When you take a step back and think about it, hiring managers and interviewers are hoping that you will prove yourself to be a fit for their tribe. Simultaneously you are looking for a (new) tribe to join and hoping to find commonality in cause and culture. This tribal mindset has been going on for nearly 400 generations. And throughout our history, humans have always fared better in a community than alone.

As humans we are social animals. We have logical brains to help us rationalize the world around us, but we do not make rational decisions. We do not act or react rationally. Instead we follow instinctual pre-programming that has been meticulously crafted and encoded into our literal genetics to help us survive. We are genetically wired to find a tribe and not get kicked out.

For hundreds more generations, survival depended on being parts of tribes, cities, feudal estates, and so forth. Now we are all part of an inescapable global tribe, plus smaller tribes that help us shape our identity in the global society. When you interview for a role, you are trying to enter a new tribe. You are being examined for worthiness within the tribe. That stress activates some instinctual behaviors in our genetic code that makes us absolutely NEED to be accepted by the tribe, whether we actually want the role or not.

External validation

Just slightly above the instinctual need for acceptance into a tribe, we also have a deeply emotional need to be graded and externally validated as a good human being by someone we admire and respect. I suspect this is an artifact of our formal education system. Many of us are wired to continuously seek a high grade or positive reinforcement of our behaviors and skills by others. And while this external validation is healthy in moderate amounts (it's always good to know what others think of us in case we have a blind spot), it can lead to over-reaching, putting others before ourselves, prioritizing our career over our family, and linking our personal sense of self-worth directly to our performance in an organization. Our need for external validation is hard-wired into us by our education system.

Addiction to mortgage payments, groceries and health care

Interviewing for an executive role is one of the most stressful things we do as humans. This is because when you look at Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs, notice that our ability to provide shelter, food, and clothing are at the base layer. And what do we need in modern society to provide those things? Money. And, generally speaking, the primary source of income in your adult life will come from your gainful employment. So when you are unemployed, or about to become unemployed, or even considering tinkering with this source of income, you can activate some anxiety that stems from meeting your physiological needs as well as your safety and belonging needs.

Add on top of that there is no true security in modern society without money. It takes a minimum amount of money to just live like a normal adult, and, worse, you must be employed to get reasonable health care in the United States. So when Americans are out of work, we are messing with our existential needs for housing, food, healthcare, safety and security, while simultaneously triggering instinctual anxiety about not belonging to a tribe.

The whole hierarchy of needs

In fact, the whole hierarchy of needs is tied to our performance in an executive interview. Personal security, resources, and health are next up, then a sense of connection, a sense of esteem, and finally self-actualization, all of which can be tied to your executive role.

A successful executive hire adds stability to the entire stack, which is why the interview feels so intensely important.

If you're successfully hired as an executive, you will be adding security and stability to the entire stack of human emotional needs. That's why it feels so intensely important to do well. But if you fail to move forward after several interviews, your entire stack of needs is threatened, causing you to feel more anxious. We then take a job, ANY JOB, to feel safe, but less than 30% of working Americans actually like what they do. So we yearn to leave this tribe for another, but the existential fear of being without tribe holds us back from leaving.

Your career trajectory is at stake

When we interview for roles above our current station, it can feel like we're being judged to be worthy or unworthy of personal growth. And while I can personally attest to this not being an accurate portrayal of reality, the emotional attachment to having the "perfect" career, or a career that only goes in one direction, up, can cause us to feel anxiety around having to nail a role we don't even want or like because that's how we keep a perfect record of growth.

Unfamiliar people, surroundings, & cultural norms

Also, when you interview, you typically are going to a place you've not been before, entering an unknown lobby, seeing unknown faces, being in an uncomfortable environment that feels slightly hostile because you don't know what natural cues and cultural references you are missing. An online interview is equally uncomfortable because you are missing up to 50% of the non-verbal communication cues that you would pick up in person. They are likely to have a hidden chat where they can communicate without including you. And they will be comfortable with each other but you won't be comfortable with them yet. All of this creates a mismatch in levels of familiarity and comfort, which can trigger anxious mindsets and nervous energy.

You haven't done this very often

Finally, hopefully you only have to interview a half-dozen or so times in your professional career. This is a skill you don't really want to practice too often as it would be indicative of not sticking to cultures and roles for very long, which is a red flag for recruiters and hiring managers. We don't interview for executive roles on a weekly basis. Not even a yearly basis. Maybe once or twice a decade is a more realistic cadence. So it's no wonder you're out of practice when it comes to interviewing, you probably just don't interview that often, and that's normal.

Limiting beliefs about interviewing

To not be anxious for an interview, you have to de-program that instinctual fear of being rejected from a new tribe and turn the experience into something else, something less existentially threatening, in order to be comfortable in the process. Or, you can turn it into a situation where the tribes are fighting over who gets to include YOU among their numbers. In either case, once you've done this, your confidence will soar, your anxiety will recede, and you will have the upper hand in negotiations with organizations wanting to hire you. Let's start the de-programming with blasting to bits some limiting beliefs around the interviewing process.

Limiting belief #1: Interviewers have all of the power

It might feel like you are navigating their world and you start out lost. We go into their building, or enter their online portal. They set the ground rules. You are in their territory. They set the conference room up so they are in numbers on one side of the table and you are on your own on the other. These things are done sometimes on purpose, and sometimes without realizing the anxiety it produces in the candidate. In either case, while the anxiety you feel in these environments is real, this does NOT give all of the power to the interviewer.

What to do about it: embrace the mutual assessor role. At the executive level, interviews are not just about the company assessing your fit, it's equally about you assessing the company's alignment with your values, aspirations, and leadership style. It's a two-way street. When you understand and embrace this two-way dynamic, you shift from a passive role to an active one. This approach alleviates some of the anxiety and positions you as a discerning leader because it puts some of the power dynamic back into your hands. Be engaged and be willing to interview them as well to make sure you'd want to work in this culture long-term.

Limiting belief #2: I need to have all the answers

When they ask you a question you don't have an answer for, you have a choice to make: make something up that sounds smart, or tell them you don't know the answer. Sometimes when you are in a high state of anxiety and feel like you need to impress everyone in the room, you'll say and do things you'll regret later. When I interviewed candidates, occasionally I would ask a question knowing full well they didn't have the answer just to see how they would respond. I appreciated the authenticity, honesty and self-confidence of those who would tell me they didn't know the answer but could go about finding out instead of making something up on the spot.

What to do about it: preparation & authenticity. While it's important to be prepared and to have rehearsed some scenarios in your head, it's equally important to be your genuine self. Executive roles are as much about personality and culture fit as they are about skills and experience. Demonstrating your ability to think on the fly vs. having a scripted answer for everything is what they're looking for. Give authentic, honest answers, as if you were already on the team and in a normal meeting with them.

Limiting belief #3: I can't admit to mistakes or weaknesses

Sometimes we get inside our own head about missteps in our career, or skills we lack, or professional certifications we don't have. While those things could be disqualifiers for some roles, having anxiety about something you can't change or control isn't going to help your chances in the interview. The thing is, those mistakes and weaknesses make you who you are and give you part of your unique perspective, along with all of the value you bring to the table.

What to do about it: have a mindset of value & contribution. Remind yourself of the immense value, experience and unique perspective you bring to the table. Think of the interview as an opportunity to showcase how your specific contributions can improve the company's mission and solve its challenges. This mindset anchors you in a position of confidence. When you approach the interview from a perspective of mutual exchange, it reframes the dynamic. You're not just a candidate seeking approval; you're a valuable asset determining the best place to invest your skills and expertise.

Limiting belief #4: My resume / CV speaks for itself

Your resume makes sense to you because you wrote it, you've stared at the document for hours on end, and you've lived the experiences inside of those lines of text. The interviewer(s) haven't. Their interpretation of what your resume or profile says may be different than yours.

What to do about it: have a conversation about who you are. It's important to know some statistical facts about your career so you can quickly highlight your background without taking a lot of time. For example, I mention that I'm a 2x venture-capital-backed CEO/founder, and I've closed $1.5B in my 25-year space/satellite career. That sentence takes less than 10 seconds but summarizes a quarter century of skill and experience. After you get the basics out of the way, answer questions with honest re-interpretations of experiences you've had. Don't memorize a story (scripted stories are painful to sit through) but instead just recall an experience and tell it like you're telling a friend or family member. At the end of every question, bring it back to focusing on you in their future, not in your past.

Limiting belief #5: It's too late for me to change or grow

Colonel Sanders started Kentucky Fried Chicken (KFC) at the age of 65. Nola Ochs was 95 when she received her B.S. in General Studies with an emphasis in history. Gladys Burrill ran a marathon at 92.

What to do about it: change and grow throughout life. Our generations (Gen X, Millennials) have grown through more technological and social change than any other generations in human history. We are highly adaptable and can embrace new technologies, cultural norms, and business models, if we want to. Just because something has worked well for you doesn't mean it's the best/only way to do something. Be willing to watch a YouTube video or do some research on a business process, technology, or communications technique that's foreign to you. For example, I had to learn how to use TikTok to reach my audience. It was uncomfortable learning a whole new user interface and cultural norm (and it's kinda hard keeping up with trends on the platform) but I've managed to amass an audience and connect with some of you who found me there in spite of being 30 years older than the target demographic.

Limiting belief #6: I have to be perfect to get the job

It's natural to want to put your absolute best forward to make the perfect first impression. And in trying to be perfect, you become the enemy of authenticity. Authentic people have flaws, make mistakes, and grow. Perfect people don't exist. When you try to be perfect, you are likely to demonstrate small self-abusive micro-expressions or traits whenever you make a mistake. These barely perceptible traits and expressions are picked up by everyone else in the room because the spotlight is on you. You'll notice they noticed, and then a cascading spiral towards falling completely apart commences. Next thing you know you're sweating, the smile isn't as natural, and you have the deer-in-headlights look on your face and can't hear anything they're saying to you.

What to do about it: just be YOU. People want to hire authentically good people, not artificially great people. The more you are yourself, the better you'll do in representing exactly who you are, and who you will be when they hire you. Relax and just talk to these people as normal people in a meeting. The more comfortable you are, and the more honest you are, the better you'll do. By a LOT. Besides, you want to make sure you'll actually enjoy the role when you're not in a highly triggered state like during an interview, so it's best to get a feel for what the culture would be like when you are naturally acting like yourself.

How to activate God-Mode during interviews

OK now for the meat and potatoes. Once you've learned why you're anxious during interviews and de-programmed all of those limiting beliefs, it's time to learn how to approach executive interviews the right way. We'll get specific about each step in the next trainings, but for now these will help you set the right frame of mind for the whole interview process on interview day.

Do the basics

  • Eat a complete breakfast, something you know your stomach will tolerate well.
  • Make sure your clothes, grooming, and hygiene (breath, hair, makeup) are all on point.
  • Be early so you're not rushed. If you're delayed, tell the recruiter immediately and give them regular updates on your ETA.
  • Bring a spare shirt or a Tide pen for when disaster strikes.

Be completely detached from the outcome

Whoever has less to lose wins the interview. If you show up desperate you will lose.

Zoom out. Like, WAY out

It's just an interview for a job. We live on a rock surrounded by a thin layer of nitrogen/oxygen hurtling through the vast darkness of the cosmos, and everyone who will remember our name will be dead in 100 years. There will be plenty of other interviews if this doesn't go well.

Treat the interview as if it were a weekly staff meeting

The hiring manager is trying to envision you in his/her weekly staff meeting. How will you communicate? What are your strengths and weaknesses as a person? Will you get along with the rest of the leadership team? Do you bring the change or stability or whatever characteristic they're looking for to the organization? Remember the interview is as much about social and cultural fit as skill and experience.

Keep the balance of power: you are interviewing them too

  • You don't want to work here if the interviewers are awful people.
  • Make sure you reserve time to ask about your working environment.
  • Why did the last person leave? What messes are you stepping into? Who influences your role? Who will you be leading? What handcuffs will your role have that you can't anticipate?

Reduce your anxiety to as close to zero as possible

  • Meditate. Listen to Calm, Brain.fm, or other meditations ahead of your interview. The closer to a zero-stress, grounded state you go into the interview with the better you'll do.
  • Deep breaths. In through the nose, hold for 3 seconds, out through the mouth. Repeat this for as long as it takes to feel that serene, steady, stable, and relaxed state of mind.
  • Visualize having a comfortable conversation with colleagues from your industry. Genuinely connect with them as humans in authentic conversation and you'll do great.
  • It's all about perspective. You are NOT in existential danger like your body is trying to signal to you. RELAX.

Be more prepared than you need to be without over-preparing

  • Know the company's history, leadership team, mission/vision/values, products, customers, competitors, etc.
  • A lot of information about publicly traded companies is available in their annual reports to investors.
  • Have a case study or presentation available in case you're asked for it, but don't volunteer it unless it's appropriate to do so. It's okay if you never pull it out, it might be inaccurate or miss the mark and it's better to hold it back if you're not sure.

Why these work

Humans make emotional decisions, then justify them with logic. If you show up excited, animated, over-anxious, too talkative, and take too long to answer questions, the interviewer will begin to worry about how to ask enough questions in the time they have with you before a hard stop to interview someone else or some other important meeting. You will have imparted your anxiety and stress onto them, and that's not what people want to feel from their leadership.

When you impart calmness, control, and executive presence in an interview, the interviewer will feel that, and they'll be more emotionally connected to you. They'll feel like they know, like, and trust you. The facts of your experience, background, professional accolades, etc., and who you are competing with for a position, are not something you can change right now, so let them be what they are. Focus on what you can influence, which is how you show up energetically.
