What you'll learn: what to do on Day One of your new executive job, how to make great first impressions with your team, and how to initiate a "listening tour."

Day one

Show up on day one refreshed and ready to start an entirely new marathon. Hopefully you took some time to rest in between roles! Your first day will look different depending on where you work, for whom you work, etc. Some companies roll out the red carpet and organize your entire first week for you. Others just plug you in and say good luck. Your mileage may vary, but be prepared for anything.

First impressions

  • Make a good impression by arriving slightly early, but not excessively so.
  • Remember to be your executive self: authentic, empathetic, audacious. Be approachable and open to conversation with everyone you meet.
  • Be sure to express gratitude and enthusiasm to everyone.
  • Stay open-minded, it might take some time to get used to the difference in pace and cultural norms.
  • Remember that you are now an executive, so act accordingly!

Stay curious

This is really important advice to heed: avoid the temptation to jump in and start doing things. You will hear problems that you know how to solve. You will see opportunities to create value and show your worth to the organization. You will want to fix everything that is different than you know it should be.

What you need to do first and foremost is listen and learn. Hear what people are saying, and take copious notes about what's wrong and how you'd fix it. But DO NOT jump in and take command, unless that is exactly what your direct supervisor wants from you. You need to establish some credibility and solid working relationships first with your team.

1:1s ASAP

Depending on how scripted your first day or week are, you want to figure out how to have 1:1 meetings with everyone on your team as soon as possible. Refer back to the Executive Management lesson for advice on how to conduct 1:1 meetings the right way. The purpose of these 1:1s is for them to get to know you, and you to know them. There are three topics that should be covered in your first 1:1 with a direct report:

  • Tell them about you, your vision & goals, what's important to you, and how you communicate.
  • Ask them about them, their goals, what's working and what's not in their role.
  • Ask them about their professional career goals and if/how they want to grow in the future.

Part of this 1:1 can also probe whether they are committed to the organization long-term or are actively searching to leave. You'd be surprised at how candid people are when they trust you, so give them every reason to trust you.

1:1s with direct supervisor & peers

You'll also want to establish some 1:1s with some other key stakeholders in your new organization to get a lay of the land. There's an internal politics chart that you can figure out, who has more weight than their title might imply, for example, and now is the time to start filling in that Influencer Diagram. Ask your direct supervisor what s/he wants you focused on for your first 90 days. Ask your peers what they are focused on both in terms of their role and their career aspirations.

Establish clear communication

As an executive, you will have a good bit of say in how people communicate with you. It's always best to adopt communications methods that are common in the organization at first, and then slowly introduce preferred communications methods over time. For example, I loathe working in organizations that rely almost entirely on email for communication, I prefer Slack/Teams type communications. But I also know that going into a company where very few people have the experience (or even the software) to communicate in those ways, I can become isolated and create unnecessary barriers to communication by implementing an entirely new way to communicate on day one. For now, you want to make sure your team understands how to communicate with you and on what kind of cadence (weekly, daily, hourly, etc.)

The listening tour

Once you have control of your calendar, you'll want to schedule additional 1:1 meetings with your direct reports, peers, and key stakeholders. This series of 1:1s I call a "listening tour" because you will go to their offices and listen to their perspectives on everything. I find it reassuring to the existing team when, as the new executive, I come to their office or cubicle or regularly-used conference room to have the initial conversation.

Your goal with this listening tour is to aim to understand current processes, challenges, and opportunities. What is everyone working on at the moment? How do they feel about the change involving adding you to the organization? What did you not hear when you went through the interview process?
