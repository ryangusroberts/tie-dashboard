How Do You Lead Your Team?

What is your STYLE of executive management? How do you actually go about managing the managers of the teams beneath you? What are the tools and methods that are best employed by someone with your personality and experience?

In this program we emphasize authenticity, which means you have to develop your own style of executive management. Becoming an executive does NOT mean becoming someone you are not.

It's about leaning into who you are when you are your highest, best version of yourself.

In this module we will discover several frameworks, some of which might fit your management style. We will also work to blend elements from frameworks to match your personality. Finally we'll talk through what I like to call "The Holy Trinity of Management", tools to effectively manage your managers and teams.

What is Executive Management?

Executive Management refers to the unique approach that you adopt when managing your team. It's your behavior and relative demeanor when interacting with others in your executive role. Executive Management encompasses the way you interact, communicate, make decisions, and inspire other team members.

Every executive leads differently. Some lead well, some not so well. How well you lead depends a lot on how well you can adapt your personal style to the stylistic needs of those around you and on your team, but also hold true to your moral core and natural personality.

Executives manage managers and larger teams by using the following methods:

  • Delegation: Assign as much responsibility and authority to your managers and teams as you can.
  • Communication: Clearly communicate the organization's vision, strategy, and expectations.
  • Mentoring and coaching: Provide guidance and support to managers and teams to help them develop their skills and hit the targets you set.
  • Empowerment: Let managers and teams take ownership of their work. Ownership increases the engagement of the team responsible for the work.
  • Collaboration: Make a place where teams can work together effectively and trust one another.

Understanding Your Management Style

To understand the key aspects of your Executive Management Style, consider these questions:

  • Leadership Approach, are you more authoritative, democratic, laid-back, or transformational?
  • Communication, do you emphasize open and transparent communication channels, ensuring that information flows freely? And do team members understand your expectations, goals, and feedback?
  • Decision-Making, do you prefer a collaborative approach, seek input from team members, or do you tend to make decisions independently? (Note: striking a balance here is key, be collaborative when it helps, but also be willing to stand-to at the wheel and drive the ship through stormy weather.)
  • Empowerment & Delegation, do you empower your team to do the things the organization needs, which fosters a culture of trust and accountability, or do you hoard all of the control and decision-making out of fear and mistrust?
  • Relationship Building, do you cultivate strong relationships by prioritizing building rapport, trust and a positive team dynamic?
  • Motivation & Inspiration, how well do you motivate your team? How do you align your team's goals with individual aspirations? Do you provide recognition and rewards to foster enthusiasm and commitment?
  • Adaptability, are you adaptable as a leader? Can you adapt not only to changing environments and technologies, but also to the ever-changing needs of your team? Can you lead different personalities differently but equally well?
  • Vision & Strategy, do you have a clear vision and think strategically about the business? Can you communicate the organization's vision, set goals, and work with your team to develop strategies that align with the broader objectives?

Executive Management Frameworks

There are some great executive management frameworks that help different executives lead in different ways. Not all of these are for everyone; rather, being exposed to different frameworks helps you pick elements from each that work best with your style of leadership.

To start out, there are some frameworks from history that don't really apply to today's workforce:

Master & Commander / Positional Authority (Level 1 Leadership)

  • Old-school military vibe (Full Metal Jacket Drill Sergeant, e.g.)
  • Prison guard / warden style (Shawshank Redemption, e.g.)
  • Entry-level / bad fast-food manager energy
  • In this framework, people follow you because they have to

If the only reason someone is following you is because they have to, they will stop following you the first chance they get. You will not attract top talent with this approach to business. You will instead be left with a revolving door of employees who come and go quickly, leaving you wondering "why doesn't anyone want to work anymore." This is quintessential "Boomer Manager" energy. Try to never rely on your positional authority.

The Five Levels of Leadership

Effective Executive Style comes from mastering higher levels of leadership skills. John Maxwell put together the best visual representation I can find of this phenomenon. The higher you go, the more people will follow you, and the farther they will follow you.

1. Servant Leadership

"Servant leadership is all about making the goals clear and then rolling your sleeves up and doing whatever it takes to help people win. In that situation, they don't work for you; you work for them." - Ken Blanchard

Principles of Servant Leadership:

  • Leaders are servants first, it's not about you
  • The desire to lead others comes from a motivation to serve others
  • Putting the legitimate needs, goals, and priorities of followers and constituents first
  • The best leaders use influence, not intimidation or manipulation, to achieve results
  • Build trusted relationships that motivate followers
  • They want things for people, not just from them

2. Mindful Leadership

"An anxious mind is an unproductive mind that doesn't inspire the best in others. A top priority for mindful leaders is cultivating peace of mind." - Matt Tenney

Mindful leadership is a leadership style in which managers learn how to consciously cultivate their ability to be present, open-minded, and compassionate while interacting with their team members, and showing the same care and consideration to themselves.

Mindful leaders cultivate: Focus, Clarity, Creativity, Compassion, Service of others.

Michael Carroll, author of The Mindful Leader, lists 9 basic competencies of a Mindful Leader:

  • Eliminate toxicity
  • Appreciate health
  • Build trust
  • Send clear messages
  • Embrace resistance
  • Understand blindness
  • Accept invitations
  • Heal wounds
  • Be realistic

3. Conscious Leadership

Our default mindset is to scan the horizon for threats. While we have few threats that endanger our physical survival, they do at times threaten the survival of our ego.

Conscious leadership is a framework in which a leader becomes radically responsible, self-aware, and focuses on building a culture of "we" rather than a culture of "me".

Below the Line: Closed, defensive, committed to being right. View life through the lens of a victim, villain, or hero.

  • Victim: People do things to me
  • Villain: I hate this person so I do things to them
  • Hero: I have to do everything myself

Above the Line: Open, curious, committed to learning. View life through the lens of a creator. Able to impact and learn from the environment around them.

The 15 Commitments. Commitment #1: I commit to taking full responsibility for the circumstances of my life, and my physical, emotional, mental and spiritual wellbeing. I commit to support others to take full responsibility for their lives.

4. Transformational Leadership

  • Encourage, inspire, and motivate team members to perform in ways that create meaningful change.
  • Develop an engaged workforce that's empowered to innovate and help shape an organization's future success.
  • Tactical leaders focus on solving straightforward problems with operations-oriented expertise.
  • Strategic leaders are future-focused with an ability to maintain a specific vision while forecasting industry and market trends.
  • Transformational leaders focus less on making decisions or establishing strategic plans, and more on facilitating organizational collaboration that can help drive a vision forward.

Build the vision, build the team, then get out of the way. Four traits of transformational leadership:

  • Idealized influence, model ethical behavior that earns a necessary level of respect and trust
  • Inspirational motivation, articulate a unified vision that encourages team members to exceed expectations
  • Intellectual stimulation, regularly challenge assumptions, take risk, and solicit team members' input/ideas
  • Individual consideration, supporting employees' individual concerns and needs

5. Radical Candor

The sweet spot between obnoxiously aggressive management and ruinous empathy on the other. Leaning towards masculine, stoic style rather than more feminine, consciousness-based styles.

This framework focuses on high-quality guidance: giving it, receiving it, encouraging it.

  • Make it personal
  • Get (sh)it done
  • Understand why it matters

This style works best in masculine environments (think software, defense industry, industrial, etc.) with team members comfortable in giving and receiving direct feedback. Can be jarring, but can also eliminate doubt about intentions and perspectives.

Other Executive Management Frameworks

  • Situational Leadership: This framework, developed by Hersey and Blanchard, suggests that effective leaders adapt their leadership style based on the maturity and readiness of their followers.
  • Authentic Leadership: This framework centers around leaders being true to themselves, transparent, and genuine. It emphasizes self-awareness, ethical behavior, and building trust with followers.
  • Adaptive Leadership: Developed by Heifetz and colleagues, this framework focuses on leaders' ability to adapt and thrive in complex and changing environments. It emphasizes experimentation, learning, and mobilizing collective action.
  • Results-Based Leadership: This approach emphasizes setting clear objectives, measuring performance, and holding individuals and teams accountable for achieving results.
  • Level 5 Leadership: Developed by Jim Collins, this framework describes leaders who blend humility with an unwavering commitment to achieving exceptional results. They prioritize the success of the organization over personal recognition.
  • Lateral Leadership: This framework focuses on influencing and collaborating with others outside of traditional hierarchical structures. It involves building relationships, leveraging networks, and leading by influence rather than formal authority.

Developing Your Executive Management Style

One thing to keep in mind as you consider these frameworks is that they are just frameworks. I like to pull elements of each to integrate into my own unique style of leadership. For example, I will be radically candid with my team and give them very direct and clear feedback (radical candor), but I also like to take a step back and realize when I am being a victim, villain, or hero and acting out of emotion instead of logic (conscious leadership). You can build your own framework from these frameworks, and many others.

As you consider all of these frameworks, remember the following:

  • You are part of the team, not above the team
  • You do management things, they do other things
  • Know and understand everyone on your team
  • Give them an environment in which they can lean in
  • Historically broken trust between companies and employees these days
  • Quiet Quitting is an organizational culture problem, not an employee problem
  • More transactional than ever in both directions
  • If they trust you and feel empowered, they will work harder / share more / stay longer
  • Be willing to help them grow their career, even if it means letting them go
  • They might be your best recommendation (or even your boss) someday
  • Be kind, but be willing to say what is needed, and change what isn't working
  • Give feedback/guidance in ways that are heard by individuals
  • Nobody should be surprised by a decision you make

The Holy Trinity of Management

Finally, I want to mention what I believe to be the foundation of good management, at the executive or any level, in any organization: Manager Tools (manager-tools.com/all-podcasts).

1. Feedback

90% of your feedback should be positive. 10% should be constructive. Give feedback daily so it's not a surprise to get feedback. One way to soften critical feedback is to sandwich it between positive feedback.

2. Coaching

Constantly be teaching your team members skills to grow as leaders themselves. Good leaders lead people. Great leaders produce more great leaders. As with the principles in this program and community, lean into helping your team grow. The very best leaders coach everyone on their team to positions above the ones they started in.

3. One-on-One Meetings

Meet with your directs regularly in a one-to-one setting. Mature teams can meet monthly, newer teams should meet weekly. Each 1-1 should be 30 minutes long:

  • 10 minutes for them to share anything with you
  • 10 minutes for what you need from them
  • 10 minutes to talk about the future, theirs, yours, the company, etc.

4. (Bonus) Delegation

For whatever reason Mark and Mike included a 4th part to the trinity. Nonetheless, delegate to your team as much as possible. Imagine they are juggling several balls, and then you drop a delegated task to them. That task is going to be a much bigger ball than the ones they normally handle, so don't load them up with too many delegated tasks all at once. The more you delegate, the better manager you are.
