Online Networking Made Easy

Broaden your reach: build the right executive network online, and use the lost art of references.

Networking defined

Networking gets a bad wrap. In fact, I don't really like using the word "networking" when I am networking. I prefer to think about things more simply: the trick to good networking is to go out there to meet interesting people, build relationships with them by learning about them and their goals, sharing yours, and finding ways to help each other.

If you go into a networking event or project with the explicit goal of furthering your own career, it doesn't work well and you don't feel great doing it. Just go make a friend or two who might know someone who wants to hire you. If you focus on building deep relationships without worrying too much about what they can do for your career, you'll come away with relationships with people who want to help your career.

The goal of networking

Generally, your primary goal is to get the right person at the right time to recognize that they need you in their organization in an executive capacity. But the goal of networking should be much broader than that. Your goal should be to meet interesting people, build good solid connections, and let those connections create new ones that might ultimately lead to the right one.

When networking online, your primary goal is to broaden your reach. When networking in real life, your primary goal is to deepen your connection.

Quantity vs. quality of connections

While online networking is about reach, our goals online are to grow our network far and fast, but also to maintain a level of quality in our network. There's not a lot of value in having 20,000 connections if 19,000 of them are not from your industry or not at the executive level. You'll have reach, but not in the directions you want to go. You want to be connecting with the right kinds of people. Your profile and your content will do some of the heavy lifting (inbound traffic) but you still need to do some of the active networking (outbound traffic) yourself.

How to build your executive network online

The thing to understand about networking is that you have to do both online and In-Real-Life (IRL) networking in this day and age. I promise you the aspiring executives competing for the roles you want are already doing this, and it's almost a sure bet that your networking skills are a big part of what's holding you back from becoming the executive you know you can be. Engagement on LinkedIn

There are plenty of ways to engage on LinkedIn. The low-hanging fruit is to read articles about topics of interest to your industry and offer insightful opinions and distillations in the comments. Doing so raises your profile within the LinkedIn algorithm. You can also join groups and be active and vocal in those communities with your thoughts and ideas about topics related to your desired role. This is an open forum for you to share your messaging. Everyone else is, so jump in too.

Beyond these low-hanging engagement techniques, here are a few that have worked for me and for people I've coached over the past few years. Some may be more valuable to you than others, so try out a few for yourself and see what feels comfortable and what works well.

  • Local Startup / Accelerator Events: You might not be looking for an opportunity in a startup, but surprisingly a number of executives get hired from these types of events. They're like old-school networking events but with a younger, edgier vibe. You'll meet some very senior people who invest in startups for a hobby. Try to mingle with the investors AND the founders a bit, you'll meet some really great people and build awesome connections.
  • Executive Webinars & Roundtables: Seek out exclusive executive webinars and virtual roundtable discussions hosted by industry associations, think tanks, or executive education programs. These often attract senior-level professionals and make for really unique networking opportunities.
  • LinkedIn Board Member Groups: Join LinkedIn groups specifically for board members, directors, and C-suite executives. These groups are more targeted and can connect you with decision-makers looking for top-level talent.
  • Virtual Leadership Think Tanks: Participate in virtual leadership think tanks or strategy sessions. These discussions delve into high-level topics and attract top-tier executives.
  • Influencer Collaboration: Identify influential thought leaders in your industry, and reach out to explore how you might put on an event together, co-hosting webinars, contributing to their content (podcasts, etc.), or joint ventures. Pro-tip: see if you can collaborate on a non-profit venture or a community give-back.
  • Cross-Industry Networking: Extend your networking efforts beyond your immediate industry. Engage in forums, groups, and platforms that cater to multiple industries. Diverse connections can open doors to unexpected opportunities.
  • Industry-Focused Podcast Hosting: Consider hosting your own podcast (audio or video) focused on executive leadership, industry trends, or strategic insights. This can attract other senior leaders as guests and listeners and facilitate valuable connections.
  • Engage with Top Journalists: Interact with journalists who cover your industry. Share insights on social media, comment on their articles, or offer expert commentary. This can enhance your visibility and reputation. I've had tremendous success writing articles for my industry trade magazines, free advertising for my companies, and it sets me up as a thought leader people seek out.
  • Participate in Online CEO Peer Groups: Look for online platforms that curate CEO peer groups or mastermind circles. These intimate communities provide a space for sharing challenges, experiences, and opportunities. Just be careful to avoid the startup-grind-hustle community who can get into a furious circle about who's grinding the most.
  • Collaborate on Industry Reports: Partner with reputable industry research organizations to contribute insights to their reports or studies. This can showcase your expertise and connect you with key players in your field.
  • LinkedIn Polls and Surveys: People love polls. Create polls or surveys on LinkedIn related to executive challenges, industry trends, or leadership topics. Engaging with respondents can spark meaningful conversations and connections.
  • High-Profile Web Platforms: If you're comfortable with public speaking, consider presenting at high-profile events like TEDx or prestigious industry webinars. These platforms can build a ton of credibility and open a lot of new doors.

The lost art (and power) of references

References are absolutely a lost art. But ask any executive recruiter and they will tell you that your References section on your LinkedIn profile is one of the first places they go. If there are dozens of relevant references with some very recent, that is a huge green flag. If you only have one or two mediocre recommendations from 2014, well, you can imagine what that says to the recruiter or hiring manager looking at your profile.

How to get great references

The simplest and most effective way I've seen work for getting references is to GIVE references. Reach out to a former colleague, a peer, subordinate, or even supervisor, and ask if you could create a reference for them in exchange for one for you. It's not uncommon for me to get requests to give references. I like when people tell me what they'd like to highlight, so don't be afraid to remind them of a project that you excelled on when you worked together.

One give, one ask: whenever you need something for yourself, it's a good idea to have given something of value first before asking for it. This is why it's so important to stay on top of your network. They won't, but if you do, they'll remember that you are the one who reaches out more often, and feel a sense of obligation to help you when they can.

What a good reference looks like

A good reference has a flow something like this:

  • Intro of you and your relationship with the individual
  • Highlight personal qualities of the individual
  • Highlight professional qualities and accomplishments of the individual
  • Summarize your thoughts and give a strong recommendation of the individual

Example reference: "I was a senior systems engineer when I worked with Meha from 2017-2022. One of the values on our engineering team is 'constant improvement,' and I've never seen anyone embrace and embody that philosophy like Meha. During the past year we've worked together, I've watched her constantly look for new challenges to tackle, whether it's getting to the root of a buggy software, writing a script to land a reservation at an always-booked restaurant, or running charity races in her off hours. She's also always the first to jump in if newer members of the team run into issues. I've loved my experience working with Meha, and I know that anyone looking to hire or work with her will, too."
