Money mindset and state management

This training provided by Ryan Roberts

Purpose: to address the psychological barriers to asking for money.

Why asking for money feels hard

The childhood programming. Most of us were taught that asking for money is rude, aggressive, or desperate. This programming runs deep and shows up as hesitation, underpricing, and over-explaining.

Your discomfort with charging is NOT evidence that you should charge less.

The worthiness question. Imposter syndrome spikes when money enters the conversation. The internal dialogue: "Who am I to charge this much?"

Your clients aren't paying for your confidence. They're paying for their transformation.

Charging equals helping, not taking. When you undercharge, you signal that your solution isn't valuable. You also attract clients who don't take action. Premium pricing creates commitment.

Undercharging is disrespectful to you AND to your client's transformation.

The 5 money mindset reframes

  1. Charging = helping. You're not taking from them; you're giving them access to transformation.
  2. Undercharging = disrespectful. Low prices signal low value and attract low-commitment clients.
  3. "No" = data. A rejection is information about fit, not a judgment of your worth.
  4. Price anchors to value. What's the outcome worth to them? Price accordingly.
  5. Confidence follows action. You won't feel ready. Ask anyway. Confidence comes after.

Pre-call state management

The 2-minute power pose. Stand tall, hands on hips or raised, for 2 minutes before any sales call. Research shows this increases testosterone and decreases cortisol.

The box breath. 4 seconds in, 4 seconds hold, 4 seconds out, 4 seconds hold. Repeat 3 times. Calms the nervous system.

The intention set. Before dialing, state: "I'm about to change this person's life. My job is to help them say yes to themselves."

Ryan: "When I sold million-dollar software contracts to governments, I learned that confidence isn't about feeling ready, it's about being prepared. You've validated. You ARE prepared. Now act like it."

Tom: "Before every sales call I pause to remind myself that I am about to deliver incredible value to this person, and if they want to continue receiving that value I'm making a sustainable offer to do just that."

Sales conversation framework

This training provided by Tom Cox

Purpose: to provide the tactical structure for moving from conversation to close, and how to handle objections. In most cases, your sales process will involve multiple conversations. This is a simple framework you can follow in one call or over multiple engagements. The process is the same regardless of how big the deal is or how long it takes to close.

Step 1: Discovery (30% of call)

This phase should be familiar. In your conversation with a Discovery coach here at Vertical Motion, we started by asking you lots of questions about you. Do the same with your clients. Be curious. Ask questions to understand their situation, pain, and desired outcome:

  • "Tell me about what's happening in your career right now."
  • "What have you tried so far? What worked? What didn't?"
  • "If nothing changes, where are you in 12 months?"

Step 2: Diagnosis (20% of call)

Use their own words to describe the problem they have, and get them to say out loud that this is indeed their problem. Reflect back what you heard. Name the problem clearly:

  • "So if I'm hearing you correctly, the core issue is [X], and it's costing you [Y]."
  • Get agreement: "Does that sound right?"

Step 3: Prescription (30% of call)

Here is where you define how you can solve their problem. Spend more time on the outcome than on the process. They aren't buying your process, they're buying their transformation. Present your offer as the solution to their diagnosed problem:

  • "Based on what you've shared, here's what I'd recommend..."
  • Problem you solve → outcome you deliver → how you deliver it → price.

Step 4: Close (20% of call)

This is where most people struggle. Remember to have the right mindset: you're helping them in a sustainable way. Ask for the decision directly. Don't ramble:

  • "Does this feel like the right fit for you?"
  • "What questions do you have before we move forward?"
  • "Are you ready to get started?"
Closing is NOT manipulation. It's an invitation to begin their transformation.

Top 5 objections and responses

This training provided by Tom Cox

Every sales conversation will involve objections. Objections are simply ways for the client to move the value equation to match the price.

Objection 1: "I need to think about it"

This is the most common objection in all of sales. It takes the pressure off the client to make a decision right now, letting them default to "no change," which feels safe in the moment. They aren't telling you WHY they want to think about it, but that's what you need to know to help them.

Response: "Of course. What specifically do you need to think through? I might be able to help you get clarity."

Objection 2: "It's too expensive"

The way to handle this is usually to help them understand the value of moving forward.

Response: "I completely understand. Let me ask you, how much is it costing you to stay where you are right now?"

This can also indicate the value equation isn't closing for them. Go through the four elements of the value equation to find which part isn't working. In my experience, it's the perceived likelihood of achievement that is the challenge here. Be ready to share testimonials or examples of past success, or reduce the price to get your first testimonial.

Objection 3: "I don't have the time"

This comes up when they feel they will have to do a lot of work to get the outcome, and get stressed about adding to an already overloaded plate. Remind them that what you do will likely reduce their time commitment long term.

Response: "That makes sense. How much time are you spending stuck in your current situation?"

Objection 4: "I need to talk to my spouse or business partner"

With the Executive Career Accelerator, this comes up on every call, and I have used it myself joining other coaching programs. Handle it one of two ways. During Discovery, ask whether their partner or spouse would support their decision to solve this problem with your help (this pre-frames the objection conversation). Or, what we do now at VM: use a 2-step process. If they want to check with a partner before committing, find out what you think their concern will be and address it now, and give them the chance to book a follow-up conversation within the next 24 hours to move forward.

Response: "Absolutely. What do you think their main concern will be? Let's address it now."

Objection 5: "I've tried this before and it didn't work"

People overestimate how many times they've tried something in different ways ("I've tried two things and I'm all out of ideas"). I like the dating analogy: they had a bad experience with a brunette and now assume all brunettes are bad partners. Find out what happened, and explain the difference between that and what you do.

Response: "What didn't go well in that experience? What would alleviate your concern about that happening working with me?"
Objections are requests for help deciding. They're NOT rejections.

Role play and hot seats: sales conversations

This session led by Ryan Roberts

Purpose: practice the sales conversation framework in real time and build muscle memory before real calls.

Part I, live roleplay. Pair up (or a founder plays prospect). One person is the seller, one is the prospect. Run a 5-minute roleplay through Discovery, Diagnosis, Prescription, Close, then 2 minutes of feedback from the group and founders, then rotate. Facilitators jump in when someone rambles or loses control, call out skipped steps ("You didn't diagnose. Go back."), and celebrate courage ("That close was clean. Do it exactly like that."). Questions after each roleplay: did you understand the prospect's pain before prescribing? Did you ask for the decision directly? What objection came up, and how did you handle it?

Part II, objection handling drill. Rapid-fire objections from founders, each participant responds in real time, and the group votes whether that response would move them toward yes. Sample objections: "I just don't think I'm ready." "Can you send me something in writing?" "What if it doesn't work for me?" "My friend tried something like this and it was a waste of money." "I've never paid for coaching before."

Part III, hot seat, real prospect review. If anyone has a real call scheduled this week: who is the prospect and what do you know about them? What's your opening question? What objection do you expect, and how will you handle it?
