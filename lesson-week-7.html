Client acquisition SOP and operating cadence

This training provided by Ryan Roberts

Purpose: to understand the cadence of acquiring new customers. Awareness, Interest, Decision, Action.

The client path (AIDA)

Every client you've closed followed the same path, whether you designed it or not: Awareness, Interest, Decision, Action. This is the AIDA model, a strategic roadmap for converting prospects into long-term partners. To attract high-level clients, aim to provide 10x the value someone pays you for. Most early-stage founders wing this. They get clients, but they can't repeat or explain how. Write it down so it runs the same way every time.

Awareness. Move from unknown entity to recognized authority in your niche. Share high-value insights (thought-leadership content that addresses specific industry pain points), optimize your digital presence so your profiles clearly communicate your value proposition, and network strategically in high-level forums, events, or podcasts where your ideal clients congregate.

Interest. Once they know you exist, demonstrate that you understand their world. Offer educational resources (lead magnets like case studies, webinars, or diagnostic tools), nurture via personalization (tailored sequences that reference their specific challenges), and use social proof that resonates with the executive level they aspire to reach.

Decision. The prospect is comparing you against other solutions or the status quo. Reduce risk and prove ROI. Conduct discovery calls that consult rather than pitch, provide a clear roadmap from current state to desired future state, and address objections around time, budget, or fit with transparency and confidence.

Action. Make the transition from prospect to client seamless. Streamline onboarding with digital contracts and automated payments, set immediate milestones and confirm the first win, and reiterate the vision to reinforce their decision.

Ryan: "In the Air Force, every mission had a pre-planned execution checklist. Not because pilots were bad, but because even expert operators make mistakes under pressure and high workload. Your acquisition process is no different. If it's only in your head, it's a habit. A habit with no redundancy."

Quick exercise. For your most recent client or prospect: how did they first hear about you? Warm relationships and direct outreach will usually dominate the awareness process for coaches, consultants, and fractional executives. That's the system you're implementing.

The SOP test. Is this written clearly enough that you could hand it to someone and they could run your outreach? If the answer is no, then you have some notes, not a system. This week you turn it into a system.

The CEO calendar

Building a CEO calendar requires a shift from a reactive "to-do list" mindset to an architectural approach that protects high-value growth activities. There are two types of time in your week. Non-negotiable: acquisition block, KPI review, one strategic decision. Flexible: everything else. Most early founders fill the calendar with delivery and admin first, and acquisition and strategy get what's left, which is usually nothing.

The philosophy of the operating cadence

Metrics provide discipline: review business metrics with the same rigor as mission hits and misses. Architecture over activity: treat the calendar as strategic architectural design, not a task list. Predictability leads to inevitability: move toward a rhythm where the right activities happen at the right time.

While cash is always king, your time, energy and attention is always your next most important asset to track and manage carefully.

Core CEO weekly activities

Strategic growth and outreach: schedule acquisition blocks first. KPI review and adjustments: regular sessions to analyze patterns and make data-driven changes. Client delivery and content creation: structure time for fulfillment and authority-building to avoid constant context-switching (the more you batch content creation and delivery, the easier it gets). Admin and personal development: set aside slots for finances, admin, and the personal growth required to lead a scaling business, such as this program.

The weekly planning ritual

Review last week's performance and analyze KPIs to see what worked and what failed. The Power of Three: set the top three priorities for the week (do this Sunday afternoon or Monday morning). The "One Thing" rule: identify the single most important objective that defines the week's success. Ensure all non-negotiables and client calls are visually blocked on the calendar, because if it's not on the calendar, it doesn't exist.

"If you let your calendar fill up with delivery and admin, you will stop growing. It's not laziness, it's physics. You can only produce what you protect time for."

CEO operating identity and retention

This training provided by Tom Cox

Purpose: shifting from being a professional for hire to a business owner.

ON vs. IN

Transitioning from practitioner to CEO is a fundamental shift in identity that requires "unlearning" the habit of doing and embracing the discipline of designing. Move from $20/hour tasks that create a "job" to the $500/hour strategic work that builds a scalable business.

Working IN the businessWorking ON the business
Delivering client workDesigning systems
Answering emailsBuilding pipeline
Being the practitionerMaking strategic decisions
Executing tasksDesigning the work itself

The ceiling of productivity: being the "best person in the room" at a task can become a ceiling that prevents the business from scaling. The 80% rule: if someone else can do the thing 80% as well as you can, that's 100% awesome. The scaling dilemma: a practitioner's focus is appropriate with 1 to 2 clients, but failing to shift by the 8 to 10 client mark means you have built a job rather than a company.

CEO time audit

Estimate the current percentage split between time doing the work and time building the business. Commonly about 80% of your time in early stages is working IN the business. Aim for a minimum of 20% CEO time initially, growing to 50%+ as the business scales.

Tom: "At 3 years and 300+ clients, I'm still spending about 60% of my time IN the business, and feel like I don't get enough time to work ON the business."

Protecting your CEO time block. Dedicate at least 2 to 4 hours weekly specifically for CEO activities so strategic work isn't pushed aside by admin or delivery. Commit to a specific day and time and protect it from interruptions. Tom keeps Mondays for this, and Fridays to catch up on anything missed during his "IN" days (Tuesday to Thursday). Develop the strategy for your content direction and sales processes rather than just executing them.

The economic value of your time. The CEO is the most expensive resource in the business. Before every task, ask the CEO question: "Is this the highest-value use of my time right now?" Every hour on low-value work is a direct loss of an hour that could go to high-value, strategic growth.

At 1 to 2 clients, mostly IN is appropriate. At 3 to 4 clients, the shift begins. At 10+ clients, if you're still mostly IN, you've built a job.

The "stop doing" list. You should be doing equal amounts of Important and Urgent work, but our natural instinct is to select Urgent far more often than Important. Action exercise: what percent of time last week did you spend working in/on this business vs. your full-time role? Of that, what % was IN vs. ON the business? Based on your honest split, identify one specific task you will stop doing or hand off this week.

Tom: "As a 3x CEO, the hardest transition wasn't learning new skills. It was unlearning the habit of doing. I was the best product developer and product manager in the room. Staying in that seat felt productive, but it was actually holding me and my organization back. The day I stopped being a product person and started building powerful product teams was the day our business started to really scale."

Client retention

Your best new client is one you already have.

Retaining your existing client base is by far the most efficient way to scale, because it drastically reduces the hustle required for new lead generation. This is about maximizing Client Lifetime Value (LTV) through intentional relationship management and strategic timing.

The financial reality of retention. Acquiring a new client costs 5 to 10x more than keeping an existing one. Said differently, a current client is about 7x more likely to buy from you than someone you haven't worked with yet. Current clients are the most reliable source for next month's revenue. Drive value by increasing the average fee, the number of purchases, or the total retention time.

The three retention levers

Lever 1, proactive check-ins. Don't wait for something to go wrong. Schedule a mid-engagement check-in and ask: what's working, what's not, what do you need that you're not getting? You ask it before they think to complain.

Lever 2, milestone acknowledgment. Be the first person to call out their progress, before they do. That's how you become irreplaceable. Clients don't leave practitioners who make them feel seen.

Lever 3, expansion timing. The best moment for an upsell or extension is immediately after a win, not at contract renewal. Trust and confidence are highest right after a positive result.

Action exercise, the weekly retention audit. Apply one proactive action to every current client on your roster this week: a check-in, a milestone celebration, or an expansion conversation.

The referral system: asking at the right moment

Building a referral system leverages the credibility and social capital you've built with clients through their success. The focus is precise timing and low-friction scripts that turn happy clients into your most effective acquisition engine.

The strategic value of referrals. Referred clients close at significantly higher rates, typically 70% compared to 30% for cold leads. They often have higher lifetime value and much lower acquisition cost, and referrals lead to faster decisions because trust is already established.

Tom: "I love referrals because my client is not only usually a great testimonial to our work, but also a source of trust that far exceeds what I could establish in an initial conversation."

Timing the ask. Ask after a "win" (an outcome the client cares about) rather than a "deliverable" (something you promised). The best time is right after a breakthrough, a compliment, or a program completion where results have materialized. Never request a referral if the client is frustrated, struggling, or if results have not yet materialized.

The referral ask script. Use a clean, direct script that acknowledges the success: "I'm really glad [specific outcome] happened. I'm looking to work with a few more people in similar situations. If anyone in your network comes to mind, I'd love an introduction. No pressure at all." Frame the request around the specific problem you solve, and reduce friction by asking whether they prefer an email intro or for you to reach out and mention their name.

Systematizing the process. Provide a pre-written intro clients can forward, maintain a referral log of who referred whom and current status, always thank referrers whether or not the referral becomes a client, and honor the social risk they took by recommending you.

Example 1: Client referral intro template

Subject: This might be for you / Introduction to [Your Name/Company]

"Hi [Friend/Colleague Name], I was thinking of you because I know you've been focused on [the problem you solve]. I've been working with [Your Name] at Vertical Motion, and we just hit a major milestone: [mention the specific win]. The results have been a game-changer for how I run my business. [Your Name] is an expert at helping people in our situation move from 'doing the work' to actually 'running the business' as a CEO. I thought you two might benefit from a quick connection. I've cc'ed [Your Name] here if you want to reach out, or you can check out his work here: [Link]. No pressure at all, just wanted to pass along a resource that's working for me!"

Example 2: The double-blind opt-in

Tom: "I learned this technique from Techstars and still use it today. It reduces friction even further to get a client to reach out on your behalf." Send an email to your client about who you are looking for and what you do, have your client forward that email to the person they intend to refer, and the referred person can choose whether to engage without the social pressure of responding to someone cc'ed on the email.

To your client: "I'm so glad you're seeing results! If anyone in your network comes to mind dealing with [problem], I'd love an intro. To keep it easy, just ask them if they're open to a connection first, so there's no pressure on them or you."

The message they send: "Hey [Friend], I've been working with an executive coach named Tom on [specific win]. It's been a game-changer. He's looking to help a few more people in similar situations. Would you be open to a quick intro email, or are you too busy right now? No pressure at all."

Action exercise, the referral outreach. Review your roster and identify one client who experienced a significant win in the last 14 days. Use one of the no-pressure scripts to ask for an introduction this week.
