The mindset of an entrepreneur

You work differently. The work you do as a coach, consultant, or fractional expert can be similar to what you do today, but that's only half the work. The other half is building and running your business. You have unlimited upside. You can make far more than as an employee, it's just not as guaranteed at the beginning. In a corporate role a grand slam is capped at four points; in your business you can hit a grand slam and score a million points. You do have some risk. If you don't figure it out and settle in for the long haul, you can go to zero, which is why we recommend building businesses that let you step back into the workforce when stability matters more than upside.

This takes time. Be patient with yourself. Starting a business takes 4x as long as you plan and costs 2x as much to build. You're learning entrepreneurship, your market, your offer, marketing, sales, delivery, and customer success all at the same time. Be resourceful. Entrepreneurs build the plane while flying it. No money for a developer? Learn to code. No time? Spend 1 hour every week prioritizing. No prospects? Learn to find customers yourself. Be resilient. Expect setbacks and expect that you'll figure it out. Your original hypothesis may change, and what you build may be very different from what you imagine now. The USMC likes to say "Improvise, Adapt, Modify, Overcome." Go with the flow.

The Revenue First Framework

The "Wantrapreneur Process" happens because people are used to working inside the end-state environment of a startup, their corporate roles. The Revenue First Framework is designed to get you out of the corporate mindset and focus entirely on what matters first:

  • Foundations: who are you, what do you want, what can you do.
  • Validation: will someone pay you for the thing you want to deliver.
  • Sales: revenue is both an investment and a validation.
  • Delivery: deliver only what you promised to your customer.
  • Marketing: once you've sold and delivered, tell others about it.
  • Scale: systematize the right way and start buying back your time.

Vertical Motion's 7 Laws of Entrepreneurship

Inspired by John Maxwell's 21 Irrefutable Laws of Leadership, these 7 laws are simpler but still complete.

  1. The Law of Cash Flow. Cash is king, always. 82% of small businesses fail because they run out of money, not because they're a bad business. Prioritize liquidity and disciplined spending.
  2. The Law of Problem Validation. Never build a solution for a problem you haven't proven exists. 35% of startups fail from lack of market need. Gather iterative feedback before risking significant capital.
  3. The Law of Purpose. Align your business with your Ikigai. Make sure there's alignment between your life purpose and what you do, or you'll create another job you start to hate.
  4. The Law of Identity. Be the CEO of your life and your business. You must adopt the identity of a person who runs this business before it can thrive. Identity precedes income.
  5. The Law of Iteration. Build the plane while flying it. Your first product should be informed by the client you signed, not your imagination. Pivot early and often based on data. Perfection is the enemy of progress.
  6. The Law of Trust Equity. The founder is the brand. In 2026 and beyond, your reputation is your pipeline. Use authentic storytelling to build trust at scale. You ARE the business. Own it.
  7. The Law of Architecture. Systematize, standardize, and automate BEFORE you scale. Documented SOPs and a weekly operating rhythm let you scale without proportional increases in stress and chaos. System creates freedom.

Offer hypothesis and ICP hypothesis

This session led by Tom Cox

An offer is a proposition to exchange value: the complete package of goods and services, the requested payment, and the terms of the agreement. It's the first thing a customer interacts with. The goal of a Grand Slam Offer is to make it so valuable and compelling that prospects would "feel stupid to say no."

The Value Equation

The Ferrari Principle: if I offered you a Ferrari for $10K, you'd find the money before you lost the deal. If I offered it for $18.5M financed over 50 years, the offer isn't compelling, because the value shifted toward me instead of you.

Value equals two terms in the numerator divided by two in the denominator. To increase value, maximize the numerator and minimize the denominator. Dream Outcome: the ultimate transformation the customer wants, driven by deep emotional desire; the more aspirational, the higher the value. Perceived Likelihood of Achievement: how confident the prospect is they'll achieve it with you, built through proof like case studies, testimonials, and guarantees. Time Delay: the gap between purchase and result; shorter is more valuable. Effort & Sacrifice: the total cost the customer must give up; lower is more valuable.

What will you offer?

What you build is irrelevant if you don't have a compelling offer. The Offer Hypothesis Canvas helps you draft your initial offer for validation testing before you talk to clients. It's a "hypothesis" because you'll validate and refine it through real conversations.

One Offer, One ICP, One Funnel, until One Million Dollars.

Who (your ideal client): their title and industry, current situation, and the trigger motivating them to change now. The problem: the ONE core problem you solve, and what it's costing them (money, time, stress, opportunity). Great offers solve one painful problem extremely well. The outcome: the tangible, measurable result you deliver and what their life looks like after. The mechanism: your unique process or framework, why it works when others fail, and your qualifications to deliver it (for example, the Whole Executive Framework from Foundations). Delivery model: consulting, 1:1 coaching, done-with-you, group coaching, or hybrid.

Pricing hypothesis and objections

Price is a hypothesis too. Start with what feels slightly uncomfortable, that's usually closer to right. Rule of thumb: if the outcome is worth $100,000 to them, charging $10,000 (10%) is reasonable. If you're nervous about your price, you're probably in the right range. Anticipate objections before validation: too expensive, no time, need to think about it, tried this before, need to talk to my partner.

Your transformation statement: "I help (who) go from (before state) to (after state) through (mechanism)." Bonus points if you can add "without X."

Submit your completed canvas before Module 1 as the foundation for validation conversations. A good hypothesis tested quickly beats a perfect hypothesis never tested. Your goal is learning, not perfection.

Your Freedom Number

This session led by Ryan Roberts

Freedom looks different for everyone. Ryan sees it as three levels: Level 1, Financial Freedom (enough money to meet your lifestyle goals), Level 2, Personal Freedom (enough time to do what you want), and Level 3, Purpose Freedom (resources to live 100% in your life purpose). Today we focus on Level 1, because you can't build on a cracked foundation.

The #1 reason businesses fail isn't a bad idea. It's running out of money before the idea works. 82% fail due to cash flow, not viability.

Before you design your offer, chase clients, or build your brand, you need to know: how long can I survive while I figure this out? That's your runway. At the end of it is your walkaway number, the income threshold that lets you leave corporate behind.

The runway and walkaway calculator

Part 1, current financial position: real monthly living expenses, current liquid reserves, additional income sources, and your monthly shortfall if you stopped working today. Part 2, your runway: total months you can operate without matching your income, your red zone (when you MUST be generating revenue), and your comfort zone. Part 3, your walkaway number: the minimum monthly income required to quit, the freedom number that replaces your lifestyle (not just bills), and the bigger freedom number that gives you options you don't even need yet.

Your walkaway number is NOT your current salary. It's your minimum viable lifestyle income plus buffer, usually 60 to 80% of current take-home.

The math: Runway (months) = Liquid Savings ÷ Monthly Burn Rate, where Burn Rate = Living Expenses minus Other Income. Example: $100K savings, well over $10,000 a yearnth expenses, $6K/month spouse income means a $9K/month burn rate, so $100K ÷ $9K = 11 months to build something generating $9,000/month before you're in trouble.

The psychology of the number. Knowing your runway creates clarity (you stop making fear-based decisions), urgency (not panic, urgency focuses you), and a target (you're building something that generates $X by Month Y). Leave this session knowing your monthly burn rate, runway in months, walkaway number, and target date. If you don't, you're building on quicksand.

Experiment mindset and risk reframe

This session led by Ryan Roberts

Ryan: "Before I built a business, I was a fighter pilot. Here's what I learned in the cockpit, especially in combat: you never have perfect information, you never feel ready, but you execute anyway, because waiting is the biggest risk of all."

You think about risk backwards. You've been trained to see risk as action ("What if I start this and it fails?"). But the real risk is inaction: what if you stay another 5 years and nothing changes? What if the right time never comes? What if your skills become obsolete while you're thinking about it?

You're not "starting a business." You're running an experiment. Scientists don't fail, they learn. Every experiment produces data.

The experiment framework

Every experiment has the same structure: Hypothesis (what do you believe is true?), Test (how will you validate or invalidate it?), Evidence (what would prove you right or wrong?), and Iterate (what did you learn, what's the next test?). In Module 1 you're not selling or starting your business, you're running validation experiments. If 10 people tell you "I wouldn't pay for that," that's not failure, it's data that saves you 6 months of building the wrong thing.

The Risk Assessment Matrix

Most of your fears live in "Low Probability + High Impact," dramatic scenarios that probably won't happen. What actually kills businesses lives in "High Probability + Low Impact": small cash flow bleeds, slow client acquisition, death by a thousand cuts. The real risks and mitigations: running out of money (know your runway, define when you'd return to corporate), building something nobody wants (validate before building, define pivot criteria), pricing too low (test pricing, raise when you have proof), and analysis paralysis (time-box decisions, use an accountability partner).

The 70% Rule: make decisions when you have 70% of the information. If you wait for 100%, you're too late. Your offer is maybe 50% right. Good enough. Test it.

Ryan: "You don't wait until you feel ready. You train, you learn, then you execute. Your validation conversations are your day sails, they reduce risk before you commit resources." Fear is a hypothesis about the future. Run experiments, not mental simulations.

Validation conversations

This session led by Tom (first 5 minutes), then Ryan

The only way to really know if the market wants what you want to provide is to ask them, through "Empathy Interviews" or "Validation Conversations." These are conversations with people who might actually buy, to make sure you understand their problem. You NEVER pitch your offer in these meetings, but you can pitch after you've validated they need it.

The psychology of listening

Here's where people fail: they listen to confirm, not to learn. You want your idea to work, so your brain filters for supporting evidence and dismisses what challenges it. This is confirmation bias, and it will kill your business before it starts. Validation conversations are NOT sales calls in disguise, pitches, or fishing for compliments. They ARE genuine curiosity about someone else's problems, data collection, and the cheapest market research you'll ever do.

The three sins of validation

Sin 1, leading the witness. Not "Don't you think executive coaching is really valuable?" but "When's the last time you invested in professional development? What made that decision?" Sin 2, pitching instead of probing. Not a 10-minute explanation of your service, but "Tell me about the last marketing challenge you faced. Walk me through how you tried to solve it." Sin 3, accepting surface answers. When they say "that sounds useful," don't say "Great!" and move on; say "Interesting. Why? What would it help you accomplish?" The real insights are one or two questions below the surface.

The validation conversation structure

1. Set context (2 min): "I'm exploring building something new and would love your perspective. I'm not selling anything, I just want to understand how you think about [problem space]." 2. Explore the problem (10 to 15 min): "Walk me through the last time you dealt with [problem]. What made it challenging? What did you try? If it disappeared tomorrow, what would be different?" 3. Test urgency and value (5 to 8 min): "How important is solving this on a scale of 1 to 10? What have you already tried or paid for? What would solving it be worth to you?" 4. Capture language (throughout): write down their exact words, not your paraphrase. This becomes your marketing copy.

Pro tip: use an AI note taker to record the conversation. These are expected and normalized now.

Identifying real buying signals

False positives (noise): "That sounds interesting," "I could see that being helpful," "You should definitely do that," "Let me know when you launch." These are polite brushoffs. Real buying signals: "How much would that cost?" (unprompted), "When can we start?" (unprompted), "I've been looking for something like this," "Can I be your first client?", plus specific descriptions of pain and emotional intensity.

If they're not paying you, their opinion is data, not validation. The only true validation is money. But enough people saying the same things is a pattern worth acting on.

Common mistakes: not enough conversations (you need 10+ minimum, patterns emerge at scale), talking to friends (they'll tell you what you want to hear, talk to strangers or professional acquaintances), not recording insights (your memory is unreliable), and pivoting after every conversation (one person is an anecdote, ten is a pattern).

Module 1 preparation and closing

This session led by Ryan Roberts

Your Module 1 non-negotiables: 1) Complete 5 validation conversations (complete, not schedule) with people who match your ICP, professional acquaintances rather than close friends. 2) Document every conversation using the Validation Conversation Guide. 3) Post your learnings in the Circle community by Friday (conversations completed, top 3 insights, one surprise, one question). 4) Connect with your cohort: introduce yourself, schedule a 15-minute mid-week check-in, and share your commitments.

How to ask for a conversation: "Hey [Name], I'm exploring building something new and would value your perspective. Would you have 20 to 30 minutes for a quick conversation? I'm not selling anything, I genuinely want to understand how you think about [problem area]."

Final mindset reset

Internalize three things: 1) You are running experiments, not risking your life. The worst case is you learn something and try again. 2) Speed beats perfection. Your offer doesn't need to be perfect, your ICP doesn't need to be locked, your pricing doesn't need to be final. Progress now beats polish later. 3) Your cohort is your competitive advantage. Use each other, learn from each other, celebrate with each other.

The $10K guarantee reminder: if you show up, do the work, and follow the process, you will make your investment back. "Show up" means attend the calls, complete the assignments, have the conversations, and take the action. This program works if you work it.
