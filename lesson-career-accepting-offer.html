Accepting the offer

It's all come down to this moment. You've rebranded yourself as an executive. You've put yourself out there to be found. You've built a world-class network and activated it to find you a role. You've had hundreds of conversations. Maybe thousands. You've interviewed multiple times. You've received the offer of your dreams. You've negotiated the offer. And now you're ready to accept the offer. Congratulations! You made it. There's just a few things left to do. Let's go through them.

Review the offer thoroughly

  • Understand all components: before accepting, ensure you've thoroughly reviewed and understood all of the components of the offer, including base salary, bonuses, equity or stock options, benefits, and any additional perks or allowances.
  • Check legal and contractual details: this includes the terms of the employment (at-will, contract, e.g.), potential restrictive covenants (like "the non's", non-compete or non-solicitation clauses), termination conditions, and severance packages.

Seek expert advice if needed

  • Legal counsel: especially for high-level executive roles, it may be wise to have an attorney review the offer, particularly if there are complex stock options, bonus structures, or legal clauses.
  • Financial advisor: have your financial advisor review the contract to understand the long-term implications of your compensation package, especially if it includes equity, deferred compensation, or other complex financial components.

Clarify doubts

If there are elements of the offer that you're unsure about or need more information on, reach out to the hiring manager, HR representative, or executive recruiter to clarify.

Craft a professional acceptance

  • Written confirmation: always accept the offer in writing. This can be in the form of an official signed letter or a detailed email. Be sure to confirm the key terms of the offer you're accepting.
  • Express gratitude: thank the company and recruiter for the offer, expressing enthusiasm about joining the team and contributing to the organization's goals.
  • Provide any requested information: this might include documentation, signed agreements, or other details the company requires to formalize your employment.

Engage with your new team

  • Pre-onboarding: given the nature of executive roles, there might be activities or engagements before the official start date, such as meetings with other executives, strategic planning sessions, or company events. We'll cover this more in the First 90 Days module.
  • Open communication: establish open channels of communication with your new employer. Discuss any logistical details regarding your onboarding, relocation (if applicable), or initial tasks.

Handle your current role gracefully

  • Provide proper notice: adhere to the terms of your current employment when leaving your job. Typically, this is two weeks, but for executive roles, a longer transition period might be required or negotiated.
  • Transition responsibilities: ensure a smooth transition by documenting critical information, tasks, and projects. Offer to assist in training a successor or providing guidance during the transition period.

Announcements

Coordinate with your new company: executive hires are often announced through press releases or internal communications. Coordinate with your new company's PR or communications team regarding the timing and content of these announcements.

Resigning the right way

Sometimes resigning can be harder than the interview process. That's why I've included this section. If you are having difficulty figuring out how you will say goodbye to your current organization and compatriots, this might help you a little bit.

Meet with your direct supervisor first

  • Private discussion: before announcing your decision to anyone else, schedule a private and confidential meeting with your direct supervisor. This should be face-to-face in person if possible, but if it has to be a video call, it should be scheduled in such a way as to ensure both they and you will have privacy during the call.
  • Clear rationale: explain the reasons for your departure clearly and honestly, emphasizing that the decision is based on personal growth or other non-negative reasons. This will help mitigate concerns or speculations about the company's health or internal issues. You want them to remember the positives about your time there, not worry about whether you see a negative trend approaching.

Offer a transition plan

  • Timeline: given your long tenure and seniority, a standard two-week notice might not suffice. Be prepared to offer a more extended transition period, during which you'll help with the handover of responsibilities.
  • Documentation: start documenting critical information, strategies, ongoing projects, and other essential details to ensure continuity. This will show your commitment to minimizing disruptions.
  • Successor training: if possible, offer to assist in the training or mentoring of your successor, ensuring they have a grasp on the key challenges and priorities of the role.

Communicate with your team and colleagues

  • Personalized approach: instead of a blanket announcement, consider first informing those directly reporting to you or critical colleagues in a more personal setting.
  • Be transparent, but positive: share your reasons for leaving in an honest yet optimistic way. Emphasize the positive experiences and growth you've had with the company.
  • Reassurance: acknowledge and address potential concerns your departure might cause. Reassure your team about the stability of the company and the transition plans in place.

Formal resignation letter

  • Professional tone: even if you've had verbal discussions, it's crucial to provide a formal resignation letter. This document should be professional, concise, and devoid of any negative sentiments.
  • Essential details: include your official end date, appreciation for the experiences and opportunities the company has provided, and a brief mention of your reasons for leaving.

Maintain professionalism until the end

  • Engagement: stay fully engaged and committed to your responsibilities until your last day. Avoid the "short-timer" mentality.
  • Avoid negative discussions: it's natural for colleagues to be curious or even emotional about your departure. While it's okay to discuss your move, avoid delving into any negative aspects or internal politics.
  • Farewell: consider hosting a farewell gathering or sending out a personal note to colleagues, expressing your gratitude and providing contact details for future touch points.

Finally, remember to leave on a high note. Your departure will be a significant event in the company's narrative, so making it as smooth and positive as possible will reflect well on your long-standing legacy and reputation.
