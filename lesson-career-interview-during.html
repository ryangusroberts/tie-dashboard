During the interview

Show up early

For virtual interviews, it's ideal for you to show up about 3 minutes early to the interview. This will give you enough time to recover from a catastrophic computer failure (reboot!) or quickly troubleshoot something like a camera or microphone issue, but you don't wind up sitting around forever or making the interviewers feel like you're desperate. Once you're sure everything is working, go on mute and turn off your camera until the first person arrives.

For in-person interviews, I like to show up an hour early to make sure I know where I am supposed to go, then go find a nearby coffee shop to gather my thoughts. I then turn up at the front desk 10-15 minutes before the interview starts.

Make introductions & follow their lead

For virtual interviews, you may be the first person on the bridge. Assume they are recording you, so make sure you've picked your nose and adjusted your makeup before you join the bridge. When someone does join the bridge, relax and engage in quick pleasantries. It's always good to ask them something about where they are, something that's happening in their industry or company, etc.

Make sure you watch for their lead. They may be stalling until a certain person joins the bridge, but as soon as that person does, they may want to shift to interview mode. Watch for these cues. It's always far easier to watch for this when they are talking instead of you, so avoid monologues here at the beginning. For in-person interviews, smile, relax, and shake hands appropriately. Did I mention to relax?

Maintain eye contact

Maintain proper eye contact with everyone through the interview. Try to establish eye contact with as many people in the room including those who are not speaking. For virtual interviews, you want to make sure you are looking at the camera, or at least near the camera, it's generally okay to look at their faces on the monitor if the camera is just above the monitor like on a laptop. You want to give them the impression that you are engaged with them and not with your own surroundings. I find that looking at their faces on the screen when speaking is far more helpful than staring at the camera. This way I can pick up on visual cues and facial expressions when I'm talking.

Posture

Sit up straight, avoid unnecessary movement, and maintain an engaged demeanor. Be physically engaged, but don't be fidgety. If you are anxious, remember everything from God-Mode Interviewing and try to put yourself in a zen state of mind.

Active listening

Make sure you understand the statements being made and the questions that are being asked by the interviewer. Write down notes on paper if that helps you process the question or information. It's always better to take notes on paper than on your computer or, worse, on your phone (yikes). Even if that is more efficient for you, it gives the impression that you are not engaged to some interviewers. Listen to understand and then respond. Do not interrupt. Do not load up an answer in your head and wait for a moment to break in. Absorb what you are hearing and contemplate what they are looking for in your answer before you start talking.

Communicate clearly

Make sure to speak clearly. Do NOT use jargon, acronyms, or slang terms. If you do have to use acronyms or industry jargon, make sure to ask the audience if everyone understands those terms. If they mention the acronym first, you can use it then. Slow down and speak clearly. Be articulate and clear in your speaking. Take a few deep breaths before you start to get yourself at the right energy level.

Common questions to prepare for

In almost every interview you will be asked a series of questions that are basically variations on a similar theme. It's a good idea to have practiced these ahead of time.

"Tell me about yourself"

Purpose: to kick off the conversation and give you a chance to set some context about yourself.

Answer: I really like to take this question into the future when asked about this. What you want to do is give your elevator pitch (from your Executive Brand), give a little bit of context about your past and present, and then switch to talking about what makes you a uniquely perfect fit for the role you're interviewing for. Focus on what's important to them, what's in the position description, what's going on in their current events, etc. A good format for answering the "tell me about yourself" question would be this:

  • Opening: talk about your current (or most recent) role & responsibilities. Focus on statistics and numbers when possible. This should take 10-15 seconds.
  • Brief background: talk about your career progression, pivotal roles and moments, achievements, etc. This should take less than 30 seconds.
  • Leadership & skills: talk about your leadership style and unique skills or relationships you possess that would be advantageous to the organization. Less than 30 seconds.
  • The future, why this role: spend a minute or so talking about why this role is intriguing to you and why you feel alignment with the organization. Demonstrate enthusiasm and excitement for the possibilities in this role. "My career has led me to this moment" is the energy you want to be putting out here.

"How do you define success in your role?"

Purpose: to gauge your priorities and leadership mindset.

Answer: speak about success not just in terms of KPIs or revenue growth, but also in building strong teams, driving innovation, creating a positive culture, and achieving long-term goals.

Example: "Success for me is a combination of hitting growth metrics consistently and developing a team that's agile, motivated, and forward-thinking."

"Describe a major challenge you faced in a leadership role and how you overcame it"

Purpose: to understand your problem-solving skills, resilience, and leadership during tough times.

Answer: tell a story about an experience you had in your career. You want to come off as candid and unrehearsed in your answer. Highlight your thought process, the resources you used, and the impact of your actions on the organization.

"How do you handle underperforming team members, especially when they hold key positions?"

Purpose: to determine your skills in managing and motivating people.

Answer: discuss a balanced approach, emphasizing understanding the root cause first.

Example: "I believe in understanding the cause of underperformance. Is it a skill gap, a motivation issue, or an external factor? Once identified, I work with the individual on a tailored improvement plan. However, I also believe in making tough decisions when necessary to ensure the overall health of the organization."

"How do you stay updated with industry trends and integrate them into the organization's strategy?"

Purpose: to assess your foresight, adaptability, and strategic vision.

Answer: mention sources like industry conferences, journals, or thought leaders you follow. Describe a specific trend you've noticed and integrated into a past role.

Example: "I regularly attend global industry conferences and maintain a network of peers with whom I exchange insights. For instance, I recently integrated AI-driven analytics into our operations after understanding its transformative impact from multiple sources."

"How do you ensure alignment with the company's goals across different teams or departments?"

Purpose: to understand your approach to cross-functional collaboration and strategic alignment.

Answer: discuss strategies like regular inter-departmental meetings, clear communication channels, and unified dashboards or tools that provide transparency.

Example: "I've found that fostering open communication, ensuring every team understands the 'big picture', and celebrating cross-team successes can significantly enhance alignment."

"How would you handle a disagreement with a senior leader or board member?"

Purpose: to gauge your conflict resolution skills and ability to navigate corporate hierarchies.

Answer: guardrails. They are looking for guardrails in how you approach conflict. They want to see confidence to stand up for yourself but also the ability to back the team even if you disagree with a decision.

Example: "Disagreements are natural and can be constructive. I believe in open dialogue, backed by data. I'd present my perspective clearly, listen to theirs, and find a middle ground or a data-driven method to assess the best path forward."

"What attracted you to our company and how do you see yourself driving value here?"

Purpose: to see if you align with the company's values and future vision.

Answer: do your homework and know the company's mission, vision, and values. Highlight these and align them with your own professional journey and strengths.

Example: "I've always admired the company's commitment to innovation. Given my experience in driving digital transformation, I see a great opportunity to further bolster this aspect and create a tangible impact."

Wrapping up the interview

Clarifying questions. Before concluding the interview, ask if there's any other information they'd like to know from you or if there are additional questions you can answer.

Next steps. Make sure to ask about the next steps in the process and what the expected timeline will be. This will help you set your expectations for what comes next and when.

Gratitude. Thank the interviewers for their time and express your interest in continuing the conversation about the position. Remember to not oversell here. Your only goal is to get another meeting, not get the job right away.

As you are leaving. In an in-person interview, if you know anyone in the company, you might ask if they're available. It's always good to have a positive connection in the company where you're interviewing. Just don't get goofy name-dropping or anything like that.
