Masterclass: Hiring & Firing The Right Way

Build and manage your team the right way: hire for fit, manage performance, and separate with integrity.

What you'll learn: how to hire the right way, performance management for executives, and firing with integrity & empathy.

One of the hardest things to get right as an executive leader is building and managing your team. It's not enough to just hire people who can do the job, you need to hire people who fit into, and can build upon, the culture you want to establish in your organization. And when it comes time to separate someone from the organization, there are good and bad ways to do that. Let's discuss all of it.

How to hire the right way

There's a right and wrong way to hire people onto your team.

Hire by asking the right questions

If you think of hiring as simply filling an open role, you're missing the point. It's important to hire the right person for a role, but their skills are only part of the math. When I hire people, I like to ask them three questions:

  • Can you do the job?
  • Will you like the job?
  • Will we like you?

The first question is about understanding their ability to perform the tasks assigned to that role. If I'm hiring a software developer, I want to gauge their acumen in programming languages and their overall way of thinking about problem solving. The second question is about making sure they will be excited and engaged in their role. It sucks to hire someone who starts out bored with the role you put them in and openly complains about not being able to do what they really want to do. The third question is really about cultural fit. Are you like us? Will you believe in our values? Will you sound and act like we do when we work together?

Adding quantitative analysis to qualitative assessment

I believe the best decisions are made when your emotions align with the data in front of you. When hiring someone, it's important to make sure they're a cultural fit and they fill the skill requirements, but there's also value in understanding the personality, core traits, and tendencies of an individual to make sure they line up with the role they're taking. This is why I love psychometrics. If you completed the Foundations Report you know what I'm talking about.

I especially like psychometric assessments like Culture Index and Predictive Index as they indicate someone's fit to a role. Are they strategic or detail-oriented? Are they social or analytical? Are they happy down in the weeds with detailed instructions, or happy in situations with lots of ambiguity? The best assessments of individuals involve multiple psychometric assessments. Interviews, references, and red flags

When you interview someone, you want to have several people review the person for different things. For example, if it's a technical role, have someone technical assess their ability. But you also want to have trusted people on your team interview for cultural alignment. References are absolutely critical to making sure the person is as they advertise themselves. Ask for some references from the candidate and actually speak to a few for each person to get a sense of who they truly are. References tend to be honest in their appraisal of someone for the work you are expecting them to do for you, but use your judgment as always.

And if you sense any red flags, if they feel 'off', or something just doesn't sit well with you, trust your intuition and be cautious. Remain objective, but also remain concerned about any feelings. Culture is the most important asset in your organization.

Performance management for executives

Once you have made the hire, you want to continuously assess all of your employees to make sure they are doing the things they are supposed to be doing, but also adding value to your culture at the same time. This is done through Performance Management.

Leveraging KPIs & OKRs from day one

Effective onboarding involves helping the new employee understand exactly what the Key Performance Indicators and Objectives / Key Results are for the role they are playing in the organization. For example, if they are in charge of Marketing, there will be several metrics they need to track in order to make sure their area of responsibility is operating efficiently. KPIs and OKRs are essential for setting clear, measurable expectations for your team. If they know what good looks like, they also know what bad looks like, so you don't have to tell them when they are not performing, the metrics will say that, and you can focus on figuring out why they are not performing. Your new hires should have a voice in what the metrics should be as they will own them going forward. If they have ideas/thoughts on ways to better measure performance, or what those metrics should be, be open to hearing them, because a sense of ownership will go a long way.

Ongoing feedback & development

In the Executive Management lesson we cover the Trinity of Management: Feedback, Coaching, and 1:1 meetings. I encourage you to go back and review those lessons, but at a high level, you want to be giving constant and actionable feedback to your employees, coach them on new skills, and meet with them regularly 1:1 to make sure they are in alignment with you. Performance issues should be addressed early and proactively with clear communication and support plans put in place. Don't let things fester.

Firing with integrity and empathy

Sometimes things just don't work out with an employee. Sometimes it is their fault, they misled you when you hired them and failed to live up to the standard, but I believe that when you fire an employee, you should feel bad about it. It usually means you let them down as a leader, or you hired the wrong person to begin with.

A leader's responsibility

When it is time to separate someone from the organization, first you should acknowledge that separation is a natural, albeit difficult, part of managing a dynamic team. Leaders are responsible for making sure the system works as effectively as possible. Sometimes, after prolonged misalignment or underperformance despite support, the only way to improve the system is to remove an individual from the team. As a leader, you should frame this decision as one made for the greater good of the team, the organization, and sometimes even the individual's long-term path.

The process matters

You absolutely need to do this the right way to ensure fairness and adherence to law and policy. The most important thing you can do is to document everything, every interaction, every reason, every example of misalignment/underperformance, every counseling, every attempt to improve results. Document, document, document. In writing, preferably with timestamps. The best way to do this is to send emails to the person you are separating and/or the HR department and/or your supervisor. Before you make the final decision to separate, it's best to conduct a thorough and objective review of the situation with your leadership team and/or your supervisor and HR department. Having multiple sets of eyes on the situation will help make sure you are not succumbing to an unconscious bias.

Firing with empathy

You want to adhere to your organization's process, but generally here's how to handle the separation meeting.

  • Be direct and clear: state the decision to terminate immediately and clearly.
  • Be respectful and empathetic: you just took away someone's resources for food and housing. Be respectful and empathetic towards the situation you are putting them in. Stay focused on the outcomes that led to the decision, not their personal failings. Do not blame.
  • Maintain dignity: allow the person to maintain their pride. Give them space for their reaction. In moments like this people react very differently. Some are shell shocked. Some are explosive. Try not to overreact to their reaction.
  • Be prepared: you will want to anticipate their questions, and clearly communicate what the next steps are, the severance package you are offering (if any), how their benefits will change/end, and if possible you will want to provide some support in the form of outplacement services.

The aftermath: protecting your team & culture

During the termination meeting, you should have your IT staff remove access to all company information and systems. You don't want to risk them copying everything down or deleting a lot of stuff. It doesn't happen often, but it's always better to be safe. After the person has left the building, you want to communicate the departure to the remaining team. Stay respectful and professional. Don't create drama or reduce your executive presence. During your 1:1s you can give space to individuals to share their feelings about a person's departure from the organization. Make sure you are also clear about how responsibilities will be delegated and if there will be a replacement for the individual in the future.
