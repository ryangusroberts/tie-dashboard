Networking In Real Life (IRL)

Deepen relationships in person: what to do before, during, and after the event.

IRL networking is a part of this process too. While online networking is about breadth of network, IRL networking is about depth of relationships. IRL networking will help you grow your executive persona, connect deeply with people who could be connected to key hiring managers, and lead to some great learning opportunities, mentorships, and friendships. Here are some tips on how to network IRL the right ways.

What to do before IRL networking events

Before you attend any events, consider these tips to be as prepared and productive as you can be.

Make your own business cards

Business cards are still a thing. I know there's all these mobile contact apps and whatnot, but there's something about leaving behind a physical calling card that's not replaceable by an app. Give people a real card and then share your @'s digitally. I have carried two types of business cards for the past decade. The first is my current company or employer's card, but the second is a business card for just me. Since my current company is deeply tied to my personal brand, I'm only carrying one at the moment, but if you are working for an employer you will want your own card. This is the card you hand out at IRL networking events to potential hiring managers or people who know them.

Make sure your card has your name, your aspirational title or role, your email, mobile, and your LinkedIn as a minimum. I recommend using moo.com, they make beautiful cards for not a lot of money, and they're fast. They do small batches (100 each) too. I would recommend using the MOO size cards, they're slightly shorter and wider than a 'standard' business card, but not obnoxiously so. I HATE getting square, circle, folding origami, or whatever non-standard cards people make to stand out. I'd also recommend Soft Touch, and go for spot gloss or raised spot gloss if you're feeling it. Spring for quality, it speaks volumes about who you are to the receiver.

Research the attendees

If you are going to an event where you know certain executives will be, learn about them ahead of the event. You can even bring a "look book" with you, which is basically a list of profile photos and names so when you spot someone you want to network with you can quickly recall their information and open a conversation with something about them already in mind.

Research attending companies

If it's a trade show or similar event, find out which companies are attending, understand their current challenges and goals, and open a dialog congratulating them on a recent achievement or ask them how they are handling a disruption in the industry.

Practice your executive pitch

The elevator pitch we created earlier should be engrained into the backs of your eyelids, and you should know exactly how to message who you aspire to be to everyone and anyone you meet. Keep a concise and compelling narrative about who you are, your accomplishments, and what you're looking to achieve. You can tailor the pitch to different people depending on whom you're speaking with.

Dress for success, but stand out

This one is a bit nuanced. You want to wear attire appropriate to your industry's standards, but also that allows you to stand out in a crowd. In my former industry (space, satellite, defense) a suit and tie are expected attire at industry networking events. However since 2010 I have opted for jeans and a turtleneck and sports coat, or a button-down shirt with a vest. I have encountered exactly two people all these years who were put off by my attire. One of them never got over it; the other acknowledged my confidence. Neither were someone I wanted to do business with.

Set clear goals

Determine the number of connections you want to make and specific people you want to meet or companies you want to target. This is especially important for introverts. You will have a limited amount of social energy so spend it wisely. Make sure you know who you want to meet and go meet them.

Prepare thoughtful questions

When you get to the event, be prepared to ask open-ended questions that can spark meaningful conversations and demonstrate your knowledge. Allow executives to share their insight, and keep adding to the conversation as it gets going.

What to do at IRL networking events

When you are at the event, remember you are there to connect deeply with people. The depth of the connections is what matters. This means you need to be fully attentive, alert, and engaged with the people you're talking to at that moment.

  • Start with your priority contacts. Approach and engage with executives or companies you've identified during your pre-event research. This ensures you connect with them while you're at your freshest and most energetic.
  • Practice active listening. It can be exhausting listening to people all day long, but that is what you're in for when you're networking the right way. Pay close attention to what others are saying. Nod in agreement, ask follow-up questions, and show genuine interest in their stories and perspectives. I find the Socratic method of steering a conversation with questions usually leads to great interactions. If you are looking around, or act checked out of the conversation, it'll lead to a bad interaction.
  • Offer value. Rather than just talking about yourself, think about how you can help others. Whether it's offering a solution to a challenge they've mentioned, or introducing them to someone else you know, be proactive here.
  • Take notes. Seriously. After each conversation, or after a handful of them, head off to the corner to jot down a few key points on the back of their business card or in your executive journal. This will help you remember the details for follow-up later. Every time I don't, I regret it because I have too many interactions to remember what I talked about with everyone, and I ultimately lose out on business opportunities.
  • Get invited to evening social events. During the course of the day, see if you can figure out who has the best social events in the evening and try to get invited to them. Whatever social energy you have left over at the end of the day, save for the evening cocktail circuit where things get a little more loose and conversational. I have found my best networking happens at these events. Customers, partners, competitors, people I want to recruit, this is where the real networking happens.
  • Rotate and move. Avoid spending too much time with one person or group. Politely exit conversations after they've reached their natural conclusion so you can maximize your time networking.
  • Stay present and approachable. Resist the urge to check your phone. Be present in the moment and appear approachable for new introductions. I strongly recommend putting your phone in Do Not Disturb mode while actively networking. When you take breaks to jot down notes you can check in online.
  • Engage with event activities. If there are workshops, Q&A sessions or panel discussions, be sure to participate actively. This not only enhances your visibility but also gives you additional time to share your talking points.
  • Introduce people and ask for introductions. If you've met someone who might benefit from knowing another attendee, make the introduction. This positions you as a connector and is usually appreciated by both parties. Concurrently, if someone mentions a person you might be interested in meeting, don't be hesitant about asking for an introduction right then and there.

What to do after networking events

After the event is over, you have some follow-up work to do to maximize the benefit and value of the event. This is where your money is made on possible job opportunities. Don't skip this step.

  • Organize your contacts. Go through your business cards and notes you've taken and prioritize the most promising connections for immediate follow-up. I like to do this the morning after the event in the hotel or airport if it's an event I travel to.
  • Send personalized follow-up emails. Within 24-48 hours, send personalized emails to your new contacts. Reference specific topics the two of you discussed to refresh their memory and express genuine appreciation for the conversation.
  • Connect on LinkedIn. Send connection requests with a customized message that again references your conversation or something specific from the event itself. Once you are connected, occasionally like, share, or comment on posts made by your new contacts. This keeps your name on their radar without being intrusive. The key word is 'occasionally.' Be careful not to give off stalker vibes.
  • Schedule face-to-face follow-up meetings. For those contacts you felt a strong connection with, propose a meet-up, lunch, or a more formal get-together. This seems almost trivial to mention, but I've had several people promise to follow up with me who just disappear until the next industry networking event.
  • Send them relevant content. If you come across an article, report or event that aligns with something that you discussed, send it their way with a note explaining why you thought of them.
  • Request an informational interview. For those who expressed interest or who you feel could offer further insights on opportunities within their company, ask for an informational interview to learn more about the company or role. Position it as a learning opportunity, not a job pitch. This is nuanced, you need to come off as someone who isn't looking for a job, but is really curious about the situation there.
  • Thank event organizers. A simple note of appreciation to the organizers can go a long way in solidifying your professional image and making you memorable to them in future events. Specifically, doing this will help them keep you in mind for future panel positions and speaking opportunities.
