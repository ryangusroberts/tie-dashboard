Mergers & Acquisitions (M&A)

The definition of Mergers & Acquisitions

Mergers and Acquisitions, also known as M&A, is a term commonly used to describe the consolidation of companies or assets through different types of financial transactions. Another term used quite often to describe M&A is "inorganic growth."

Mergers: When a merger happens between two companies, they combine together into a new entity and both of the previous entities cease to exist.

Acquisitions: An acquisition happens when one company buys the assets or the stock of another company. In this case, the company that is acquired ceases to exist, and the acquiring company continues on.

Why companies perform M&A

There are a lot of reasons that companies will merge with and/or acquire other companies, and most of them make sense on the surface.

  • Mutual Benefit: M&A can increase the value of the new business entity. In other words, putting two things together can sometimes create something that's worth more than both of those original things combined. Think "chocolate & peanut butter," or 1 + 1 = 3. This usually shows up as either increased revenue, decreased cost, and (usually) increased profitability.
  • Diversification: Another reason for M&A is to diversify a company's exposure to new markets, or adding new products/services to their existing market. It spreads the risk associated with the company around to avoid disaster in case the main business changes rapidly.
  • Acquisition of Assets: Something very commonly seen in tech, larger companies will acquire smaller, younger companies that have unique intellectual property or product development teams that can do things the larger company can't (because of the skill required, or because of patents / protections).
  • Growth: By acquiring or merging with competitors, companies can increase their share of market overnight and make business development processes simpler. If you can't beat them, join them.
  • Supply Chain Power: Acquiring a competitor also means now only one company is buying supplies instead of two, which gives that company more buying power overall. Additionally, by purchasing a supplier, the company can eliminate the margins that the supplier previously added to its costs, while also securing their own supply chain and disrupting that of their competitors.

The M&A process

Generally the process for all M&A is similar. It starts by understanding why the M&A is needed, finding targets, due diligence and negotiation.

  • Strategy: The acquiring firm determines what the purpose of M&A would be for them. Many times larger companies will have purpose-built teams who do this, but smaller companies will leave this as a responsibility for the leadership team.
  • Finding Targets: Once the acquiring company has developed a strategy for inorganic growth, they need to find companies, teams, or assets to acquire. This involves a bit of research but also testing the waters with potential targets.
  • Due Diligence: Once a target has been identified, the M&A team will start the kabuki dance of wooing the target to want to be acquired. Typically this looks like entering into a "Letter of Agreement" or "Notice of Intent" that allows the acquiring company to really dig in to the target company's financials, their processes, and actually see their real challenges. During this period the target's valuation (how much it would be worth in a deal) is also determined and agreed upon (or not, when the deal doesn't work out).
  • Negotiation: Once the target's valuation is agreed upon, the risks are all well-understood by both sides, and everyone is in general alignment on what should happen, the financial side of the deal gets figured out. M&A can involve paying cash for the value of the target, trading shares of the target company's stock for the acquiring company's stock, or anything in-between. In many cases, the leadership team and the ownership team of the target company are "bought out," meaning they get a nice check in the mail and no longer have anything to do with the business. In other cases when the leadership team is critical to making the M&A work, they will be given package deals that keep them engaged for 2-3 years while their successors are found, trained, and installed.

Challenges of M&A

On average, about 75% of M&A deals fail. Failure means the M&A didn't lead to the expected return on investment. Sometimes this failure is spectacular (like when Daimler and Chrysler merged in 1998), other times it's just disappointing results. Moreover, about 95% of all M&A ideas never come to fruition. It's very common to have two sides discuss a merger or acquisition that never even gets to Due Diligence.

  • Integration Difficulties: Integrating the two businesses can be challenging, especially if there are major cultural or location differences. There needs to be a consolidation of leadership, processes, and systems. Simply layering the acquiring company's way of doing things on top of an acquired company can destroy the value of that company.
  • Cultural Clashes: Quite often, the two companies coming together will have different beliefs and values. If the two cultures can't quickly blend and merge together, the value will be destroyed.
  • Overpayment: This is the biggest reason most M&A deals fail. The optimism of the M&A deal and all the things it could lead to are often much higher than the actual return because of the inability to integrate the two entities in perfect alignment. In 2001, AOL acquired Time Warner for $147 billion, however the following year reported a write-down of $99 billion, the largest annual net loss ever recorded.
  • Regulatory Issues: Sometimes acquiring a competitor can lead to antitrust issues. For example the US Department of Justice blocked the merger between AT&T and Time Warner due to the fact that it would create a monopoly, which is expressly forbidden in US law.
  • Poor Due Diligence: Sometimes the target company hides some of its skeletons really well. Sometimes the acquiring company just didn't ask all the right questions. Sometimes the M&A team uses assumptions that aren't correct that lead to surprises post-acquisition. This is why due diligence is so critically important to the success of any M&A deal.

Key Negotiation Concepts

Why negotiation is important for executives

Negotiation is crucial for executives in: Deal-making (mergers, acquisitions, partnerships, contracts), Resource Allocation (budgeting, staffing, project prioritization), Conflict Resolution (internal disputes, external disagreements), Leadership (influencing teams, motivating stakeholders), and Compensation Negotiations (for themselves and their teams).

Defining business negotiation

Negotiation is a process of reaching an agreement between two or more parties with different interests. One party needs something the other party has, and usually both need what each other has. The fair exchange of value has to be determined and agreed to by both sides of the deal, and this is where most deals fall apart.

One of my favorite terms from my MBA program was BATNA, the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. This is your walk-away point. What's the best you can do if no agreement is reached? A strong BATNA empowers you to be bold in your negotiations. A weak BATNA can lead to a significant loss in value along the way. For example, having three job offers on the table at one time gives you a strong BATNA, you can negotiate with the deal you want the most while knowing you'll still have a new job somewhere else. A weak BATNA might look like being on the outside of an airlock running out of oxygen, trying to convince HAL to open the pod bay doors.

The Zone of Possible Agreement (ZOPA) is the range where the two sides overlap in terms of perceived value. The settlement ranges are what each party believes would be the best outcome and the worst outcome to make the deal work. Beyond the worst outcome is the BATNA for each party.

Negotiation styles

This is where your personality, patience, and experience can really help you be successful in business, or keep you stuck.

  • Win-Lose (Distributive): Focusing on maximizing one's own gains, oftentimes at the expense of the other party. This is an effective one-time, transactional negotiation, but you will burn all good will with the other party by 'running up the score' on them. This is usually reserved for time-sensitive negotiations involving scarce resources, like price gouging.
  • Win-Win (Integrative): Almost certainly my favorite type of negotiation is when both parties gain more value than they give up. If you join the Executive Career Accelerator and make 10x the cost of joining back in your new executive career, we both won in that case. Note that win-win deals require a high degree of trust and cooperation, and can be time-consuming. I have made an entire career on approaching things this way.
  • Compromising: This is about finding a middle ground where each party gives up something. This can be a quick way to reach an agreement, but may lead to less value on the table for everyone, and it doesn't always address the underlying needs of one or both parties. This is used when time doesn't allow for the win-win approach, or the two parties are at an impasse and any deal is better than no deal, or when a temporary solution is needed to hold things together or move forward.
  • Avoiding: Sometimes the only winning move is not to play. This can be appropriate if the issue is trivial or the relationship is not important, but it can damage relationships and make the other party feel unheard. I tend to do this when emotions are running high or the cost of the negotiation outweighs the benefits.
  • Accommodating: This approach is about yielding to the other party's demands. An unconditional surrender in war would be an example here. This works to preserve the relationship, especially if you were in the wrong in the first place. Be careful not to be manipulated into this frame of mind, people who negotiate in bad faith will try to put you here to get the most out of you (win-lose).

Negotiation tactics

There are elements of the negotiation process that can help you have a successful negotiation. Preparation, Patience, and Persistence are Paramount.

  • Preparation is the foundation of successful negotiation. Research: know the other party, their interests, and their BATNA. Set clear goals: what do you want to achieve, and what are your must-haves vs. your nice-to-haves? Develop a strategy: how will you approach the negotiation?
  • Active Listening is how to actually hear the needs and desires of the other party. Pay attention to what they are saying, both verbally and nonverbally. Ask clarifying questions to ensure you understand their needs and concerns. Summarize to show that you are listening and understanding.
  • Framing: present your position in a way that is persuasive to the other party. Focus on mutual gains: how will your proposal benefit them? Use data and evidence to support your claims. Control the narrative: don't let the other party frame the discussion in a way that disadvantages you.
  • Anchoring is setting the initial offer or expectation. When you go into a car dealership and see the list price for a car you want to buy, that is the anchored price. A well-placed anchor can influence the entire negotiation.
  • Building Rapport: establish a connection with the other party. Find areas of mutual interest and belief, build a relationship based on trust and empathy. People are more likely to agree with (and help) people they know, like, and trust.

Handling difficult tactics

Sometimes the other party will attempt to manipulate the negotiation. Sometimes the other party is just an asshole.

  • Aggressive behavior: Stay calm and assertive. Don't be afraid to walk away. Know your BATNA and don't go below it.
  • Lowball offers: Counter with a realistic offer and explain your reasoning.
  • Ultimatums: These are designed to create Fear Of Missing Out (FOMO). Question the ultimatum and explore some alternatives. Don't be afraid to say no.

Corporate Financial Literacy

I have learned that you don't need to be a financial expert to be a senior executive in a large corporation. But you do need to have some understanding of how finance works. Said differently, you don't need to know how to create financial reports, but you better know how to read them. More importantly, you need to know what to track and what's good/bad.

Defining corporate finance

The simplest definition of corporate finance is that it is the function of managing the organization's cash and stored value. It's about making smart decisions on things like:

  • Managing day-to-day finances (making sure the company never runs out of money)
  • Raising capital (how the company gets the money it needs to grow)
  • Investing in projects (deciding which projects or initiatives to invest in)
  • Balancing risk and return (making decisions that offer the best potential return while managing the associated risks)

Why financial literacy matters for executives

  • Informed Decision-Making: evaluating investment opportunities (is this project financially viable, what's the ROI?), assessing risk, resource allocation across departments or projects, and pricing strategies.
  • Performance Measurement: monitoring KPIs (are we meeting revenue and profit targets?), identifying trends (are sales growing or declining, and what are the key drivers?), and benchmarking against competitors.
  • Communicating with Stakeholders: investors (demonstrating financial health and growth potential), analysts (clear explanations of results), the board of directors (financial reports and Q&A), and employees (sharing relevant information to build trust).
  • Career Advancement: executives who understand finance are seen as more credible and capable.
  • Personal Finance: the principles of corporate finance can also be applied to personal financial management.

Key financial statements

There are three financial statements managed by corporate finance that every executive should know how to read: the Balance Sheet, Income Statement, and Statement of Cash Flows. The Balance Sheet is a moment in time, whereas the Income Statement and Cash Flow Statement are recordings of what happened over a period of time.

Balance Sheet. This is a snapshot in time of the company's financial position at any given moment. Typically recorded on a monthly, quarterly, or annual basis depending on the size of organization. The purpose is to understand the overall health of the business. Does it have enough assets to cover its liabilities? Is it increasing or decreasing in value over time? There are 3 elements:

  • Assets are what the company owns. This can be physical things, virtual things (like website domains and software products), or even 'goodwill' which is value that can't be physically or virtually sold off (like the brand value of Nike).
  • Liabilities are what the company owes. These are the debts, accounts payable, lines of credit, and loans of the business. They are divided into short-term debts and long-term debts.
  • Owner's Equity is the value of what's left after liabilities are subtracted from assets. And yes, it is possible to have negative equity. It's a fancy way of saying 'net value' of the enterprise.

Income Statement. Also called the Profit & Loss Statement or P&L, it is a recording of the economic activity that took place over a certain period of time (a month, a quarter, and a year). It lists all of the revenues brought into the business at the top, and then subtracts the expenses incurred in that same timeframe. The primary sections are:

  • Revenues: the money brought into the company through sales
  • Cost of Goods Sold (COGS): what it cost to fulfill those sales
  • Gross Profit: basically Revenue minus COGS, the money left over after fulfilling delivery
  • Operating Expenses: the day-to-day and one-time expenses incurred by a business that are not directly attributable to the delivery of the products/services
  • Operating Income: the money left over after COGS and Operating Expenses are subtracted from Revenue. Also referred to as EBITDA, which stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. Sometimes just called EBIT.
  • Interest, Depreciation, Amortization, Taxes: the non-operational costs incurred by the business such as interest payments on debt, the depreciation of assets (like trucks), and taxes paid to federal, state, and local entities.
  • Net Income: the amount of money left over after ALL expenses are deducted from the revenues in that period of time.

Statement of Cash Flows. Shows the movement of cash into and out of the company during a specific period of time, usually in alignment with the Income Statement. The purpose is to understand how the company generates and uses cash. Is the company generating enough cash to meet its obligations? There are three key categories:

  • Operating Activities: cash that comes in and goes out due to the normal course of business. This is the majority of the money in and out of the business.
  • Investing Activities: cash that comes in or goes out due to investments the business is making for the long-term, or income that comes from a source that's not the primary focus of the business.
  • Financing Activities: the cash that goes in and out due to borrowing or lending money.

Examples of financial statements (ExxonMobil, FY2023)

Balance Sheet (as of Dec. 31, 2023): total assets were $376.3 billion; total liabilities were $163.8 billion; total equity was $212.5 billion; total liabilities and equity were $376.6 billion, which equals the total assets for the period.

Income Statement: total revenue was $344.6 billion; total costs were $291.8 billion; net income or profit was $36 billion.

Cash Flow Statement: operating activities generated a positive cash flow of $55.4 billion; investing activities generated cash outflows of -$19.3 billion (additions to property, plant, and equipment made up the majority, meaning the company invested in new fixed assets); financing activities generated cash outflows of -$34.3 billion (dividends paid to shareholders and acquisitions of common stock comprised most).

Key financial ratios

Financial ratios are really useful in determining the trend of a business. Compared over time, these ratios and metrics can help you understand if a business is growing or dying.

  • Profitability Ratios measure the company's ability to generate profits. Gross Profit Margin (GM%): how much profit is made on each dollar of sales after deducting the cost of goods sold. Net Profit Margin (NI%): how much profit is made on each dollar of sales after deducting all expenses. Return on Equity (ROE): how effectively a company is using its shareholders' investments to generate profits.
  • Liquidity Ratios measure the company's ability to meet its short-term obligations. Current Ratio: can the company pay its short-term bills with its current assets? Quick Ratio (Acid-Test): a more conservative measure of liquidity that excludes inventory.
  • Solvency Ratios measure the company's ability to meet its long-term obligations. Debt-to-Equity Ratio: how much debt does the company have relative to equity?
