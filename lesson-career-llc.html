Forming Your Own LLC, How & Why

The entity that carries your fractional practice, covers gaps, and gives you a second lane of income.

Career trackSelf-paced

Lesson recordingCareer
Why this is in the Career track: this is your cross-leverage move. Even if your dial points at Career, standing up an LLC gives you the second lane, a layoff becomes a pivot, not a cliff, and your next review has real leverage behind it.

The reality of the 2020s is that layoffs, right-sizing, outsourcing, cost-cutting, and maximizing shareholder value above all else has led to an incredibly fractured, chaotic, and transactional job market. For that reason alone, you should always have additional ways to earn income besides your primary job in case you are sent home with a box one day. But there are several other reasons to have options as an executive.

Why to form an LLC

  • Bridging Gaps: An LLC allows executives to seamlessly transition between corporate roles by providing a platform for independent consulting. If you lose your job, or have a gap in time between jobs, you can quickly spin up some consulting opportunities to avoid making $0/month during that time and extend your financial runway. Having an LLC also covers up any questions about 'gaps' on your resume.
  • Enhanced Flexibility: With an LLC, executives can pursue consulting opportunities that align with their expertise and interests, offering greater control over their career trajectory. You can consult with organizations who need what you do in an industry that is not competitive with your full-time organization. This is called "moonlighting."
  • Financial Security: In times of layoffs or unforeseen circumstances, an LLC provides a stable foundation for generating income and maintaining financial stability. Having the ability to earn income that is separate from your full-time work helps spread out the risk of your primary source of income. You might make great money, but if you don't have six months of runway saved up, doing some consulting work might be able to help fill the financial gap if something happens to your full-time job.
  • Tax Advantages: LLCs offer flexible tax options, allowing executives to optimize their tax burden and maximize their earnings. You can run a lot of expenses through your business in order to reduce your total net income.

How to form an LLC

Here is the (simplified) process of forming an LLC in the United States. You might want to consult a lawyer, tax accountant, or financial advisor if you run into anything that is confusing or feels different than what I describe below.

Choose a name

Select a unique and professional name for your LLC that reflects your expertise and brand. You can usually search the state database of businesses to see if your name is available. The LLC name needs to fit a few criteria:

  • Must be distinguishable
  • Must include specific terms or abbreviations: Limited Liability Company, LTD., LLC, etc.
  • Capitalization doesn't matter but punctuation does

State of formation

If you are a US Citizen or Permanent Resident you can form an LLC in any of the 50 states. Determine the most advantageous state to form your LLC. Consider factors such as fees, taxes, regulations, and simplicity. I generally encourage people to form their first LLC in the state in which they currently live. You may want to form an LLC in Delaware for more law protection, or Wyoming to shield the ability to know who owns the LLC, etc. My LLC was formed in Georgia when we lived there, and I migrated it to Colorado when we moved here.

Registered agent

You need to appoint a registered agent to receive legal and official documents on behalf of your LLC. This is where lawsuits, summons, court orders, etc. will go. There has to be a live person who has normal working hours available to accept these kinds of documents that need to be signed for. You can appoint yourself (easiest approach) or you can work with a professional Registered Agent organization.

Operating agreement

Next you need to create a comprehensive operating agreement that outlines the ownership structure, responsibilities, and operating procedures of your LLC. Most states have an automated process to create these, however a template is provided so you can create one on your own (without paying LegalZoom for it).

Articles of organization

Again, most states have an auto-fill form that you just fill in a few answers and it generates for you automatically. A template is also provided just in case.

Obtain an EIN from the IRS

Next you need to secure an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) for tax purposes. Getting the EIN is relatively easy. Be sure to do this between Monday and Friday, 7AM to 10PM (for whatever reason this is the only time you can apply for an EIN on their website). Use the IRS "Get an employer identification number" tool to get one directly from the IRS in minutes for free.

Open a business bank account

You need to establish a separate bank account for your LLC to maintain separation between your personal and business accounting. This is really important, do not skip this step. You can get free business checking accounts from Chase, Bank of America, Credit Unions, etc. Your tax accountant will thank you next spring.

Comply with regulations

You must ensure that your LLC complies with all applicable state and federal regulations, including licensing and reporting requirements. There are special situations (like political and lobby situations, or working in a protected field) that might require some legwork to make sure you're good to go.

Other tasks & considerations

There are a few other things you can and should do when you form your LLC. These are optional, but if you want to get up and running as a consultant these will be very helpful.

  • Get a business credit card. I recommend the Chase Sapphire card or the American Express Gold Card for Business as good options for businesses that have little to no credit yet. Running your expenses through the credit card makes it easier to track your expenses, helps you build credit for your business, and hopefully earns you some rewards / points.
  • Design your company brand & logo. I spent $50 on Fiverr for the logo here at Vertical Motion. It doesn't have to be expensive; it just has to exist. Keep it simple and clean, and make it feel professional.
  • Get your domain name. Once you know the name of your company, you should acquire a domain name for your business. I generally recommend buying a .com domain name, and I also don't think it's necessary or even prudent to spend hundreds or thousands of dollars on a domain name that matches your company perfectly. Our company is called Vertical Motion, and our domain name is vmleader.com.
  • Build your company website. Websites add legitimacy to businesses. Using GoDaddy, Wix, or some other simple web editing software you can quickly put up a consulting website. You only need one page that explains what kind of consulting you do and how to contact you.
  • Set up a work email address. You can also set up an email address using your new domain name. Microsoft, Google, and dozens of others have systems that do this very easily.
  • Set up a work phone number. If you use Google for Business, you can get a Google phone number, which is a virtual phone number that can be forwarded to your personal phone number. That way your personal number isn't out there for the world to see. If you start getting spam calls to your work number, you can simply change it, or have it go to digital voicemail instead of ringing your phone.

Define your consulting offer

Once you have all of that set up, you can get clear about what it is you'll actually be consulting. Generally I recommend sticking to your highest, best skills. For example, if you were in marketing in the automotive industry, you can be an automotive marketing consultant helping companies in that industry.

Create a consulting agreement

The one and only time you really need to speak to an attorney is when you start creating your consulting agreement. There are plenty of templates out there, but this is a case where having an attorney draft one up for your specific needs might make sense. I have an attorney who charges $400/hr and does amazing work helping me. See if you can find attorneys that work with startups and small businesses. This also might be the one time I'd consider LegalZoom as an option.

Set your pricing

Know your rate. Whether it's an hourly rate, daily rate, or monthly rate, be clear about what that rate is. If you need to make $15K every month and have the capacity to work with 3 consulting clients, well, $5K/month seems like a reasonable rate. But if what you do is very specialized and high-level, then consider raising that rate so you can bank some runway in between roles.
