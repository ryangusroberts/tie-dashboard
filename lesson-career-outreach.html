What you'll learn: how to connect with executive recruiters, how to connect with executives at target companies, and tools & scripts to track your outreach progress.

Now let's focus on how you actually make the connection with people who can influence your career in a positive way. We'll start with recruiters, then talk about hiring managers. There are some useful tools at the end of this training to help you stay organized.

I. Connecting with executive recruiters

Executive recruiters provide the bridge between the hidden job market and the executives companies are seeking. They specialize in sourcing and placing top-tier talent for senior-level and executive positions, such as CxO, Board Members, and Vice Presidents.

Unlike regular recruiters, who often cast a broad net for a multitude of roles across various levels, executive recruiters operate within a more niche space, often focusing on a specific industry or set of roles. For example, it would not be uncommon to meet an executive recruiter who only places technical talent at the VP and CTO levels.

Executive recruiters delve deeply into understanding not just the role, but the intricate dynamics of the company culture, business model, and industry trends. This helps ensure that candidates don't just fit the role technically, but also align with the company's vision and ethos. They also cultivate long-lasting relationships with industry leaders and potential candidates, sometimes over years. This ensures a trust-based rapport and an understanding of a candidate's career trajectory and aspirations.

Each executive recruiter has their own style and employs various strategies, from conducting in-depth interviews to assessing cultural fit. And each search is unique to the company, role, and candidates. Beyond recruitment, executive recruiters tend to play an advisory role to organizational leaders, offering insights on market trends, compensation benchmarks, and leadership strategies. This is why you may occasionally be 'vetted' by an executive recruiter who was not engaged in presenting you to a company.

So while regular recruiters focus on velocity of transactions, executive recruiters prioritize precision, alignment, and long-term value, ensuring the leadership roles they fill have a transformative impact on the organizations they serve.

I have had the fortune of connecting with several executive recruiters over my career. I am still very close to many of them. At some point you will get to meet and work with at least one executive recruiter. When you do, be sure to ask them how they like to learn about you and how you can best support them. I highly encourage you to stay connected with these fantastic human beings over time. When you are the CxO, you will want to rely on them to help place VPs under you in your organization.

What executive recruiters need from candidates

When you work with an executive recruiter, you need to understand their process so you can help them do their job more easily. They want to place you. Help them do it. Think of executive recruiters a little bit like your lawyer: they want to place you but they want to place you in the RIGHT role at the RIGHT organization. You are building a long-term relationship that will span decades if you do it right, but if you lose their trust, you will have seriously damaged any chance you have to work with them again, and the companies they support.

So that being said, here's how you work with executive recruiters:

  • Complete transparency (details): offer a comprehensive career history, including any gaps or significant changes. Be candid about successes, failures, and lessons learned.
  • Complete transparency (compensation): be open about current compensation packages and your expectations. This helps recruiters gauge alignment with the employer's budget.
  • Long-term vision: share your professional aspirations, detailing where you see yourself in the next 5-10 years. This ensures alignment with the company's trajectory and the role's potential growth.
  • Availability & responsiveness: recruiters work within tight timelines. Promptly respond to their emails, calls, and requests. If you're not interested, communicate that clearly so they can move on efficiently.
  • Detailed references: provide comprehensive reference lists, including past supervisors, peers, and subordinates. This offers a 360-degree perspective on your leadership style and impact.
  • Cultural insights: share experiences and preferences regarding company culture, work environment, and team dynamics. This assists in gauging fit beyond the technical requirements of the role.
  • Honest feedback: after interviews, offer genuine feedback on your perception of the role, company, and any concerns. This helps recruiters manage expectations and address potential issues.
  • Preparedness: come well-prepared for interviews, having researched the company, industry, and its challenges. Showcase strategic thinking, not just past achievements. We'll talk more about this in the Interviewing module, but DO NOT show up unprepared for an interview. It will end badly for everyone involved.
  • Openness to development: if a recruiter suggests certain courses, certifications, or coaching, be open to them. They often have a pulse on skills or traits in demand.
  • Portfolio/work samples: where applicable, like for roles in strategy, marketing, or product development, offer samples of past work, strategies devised, or projects led.
  • Collaboration: treat the recruiter as a partner. Regularly update them on any changes in your career or preferences and solicit their advice on career moves.

Ways to connect with executive recruiters

The easiest way to connect with executive recruiters is to search for them online, primarily in LinkedIn. Simply search "executive recruiter" and "your industry" or "your area" and several should come up quickly enough. That being said, there are some other ways to find executive recruiters that might not seem so obvious. Each of these methods are ones that I have used or I know others have used.

  • Exclusive networking events (private clubs and associations): some cities (like St. Louis, Chicago, NYC, etc.) have private clubs or associations where senior executives and business leaders mingle. Being active in such places can lead to organic encounters with recruiters.
  • Exclusive networking events (charity galas and boards): many top executive recruiters are involved in charitable work. By serving on boards or attending high-profile charity events, you can mingle with a different class of professionals, and be seen as an up-and-coming potential executive.
  • Personal introductions: leverage your network of peers, mentors, or industry leaders who may have personal connections with top executive recruiters. A warm introduction from a trusted associate is invaluable.
  • Become a thought leader: regularly publish content, offer to speak at elite industry events, or even consider writing a book. Recruiters respect those who are recognized industry leaders. This is why we focus a lot on thought leadership in this program, and why all of the major executive placement firms focus on it too.
  • Engage in industry think tanks: like thought leadership, participation in industry-focused think tanks and panels can place you in rooms with other industry leaders.
  • Get used to playing golf: I personally don't enjoy golf but I will play when I need to network. Activities like golf tournaments, art auctions, and horse races often attract high-profile business people.
  • Engage with boutique executive search firms: while everyone knows the big names, niche or boutique firms often have deep connections within specific industries and can offer a more personalized approach.
  • Join startup advisory boards: joining advisory boards, especially for startups in cutting-edge industries, can get you noticed. These roles often lead to discussions about talent and executive needs, bringing you closer to recruiters. This is why I mentor for both Techstars and Patriot Boot Camp.
  • Connect through alumni circles: engage actively in alumni events from your alma mater, especially if you've graduated from prestigious institutions. Recruiters keep close ties with elite schools to find potential talent.
  • Offer mentorship: by mentoring younger talent or being a part of executive mentorship programs, you'll indirectly make connections with senior professionals and possibly recruiters.
  • Regular check-ins: if you've previously had interactions with executive recruiters, check in with them occasionally, updating them on your career progression and seeking industry insights. Jumping to top of mind once in a while is a good way to make sure you are considered when a role comes up that might suit you.

II. Connecting with executives at target companies

We talked earlier about how we want to deploy inbound marketing and outbound marketing strategies to help you connect with a role that suits you. Let's explore how both strategies work in connecting with the hiring managers and senior leadership of the companies you are targeting.

Let's use Tinder as an analogy. Your profile on Tinder is like our thought leadership content and our LinkedIn profile. But 'swiping right' and sending hearts or fist bumps or whatever to another user that's attractive to you is like targeted outreach. The difference here is you have to do some legwork in finding both companies that are a fit and the hiring managers there, as opposed to just swiping right (wouldn't it be cool if LinkedIn had a feature like that!?)

Inbound: attracting executives to you

The whole purpose of our executive brand, our elevator pitch, the work we do on our LinkedIn profile and our resume / CV, and our thought leadership content strategy, is to attract the right people into our world. Specifically we want to attract these three types of people in this order:

  • People who can hire you
  • People who influence people that can hire you
  • People who know people that can hire you

Let's assume there are 100 companies in the world that are looking for a candidate and are a perfect fit for the executive you want to become. That means there are likely 100 hiring managers in the world who are looking for you. There are 900 million members on LinkedIn. So to find those 100 people we have to get really targeted in our messaging so they find us organically through the LinkedIn algorithm. We also need them to find us BEFORE they find someone who's as good a fit as we are. And we need to stand out above the others they found before they found us.

It's all about thought leadership

I know first-hand putting yourself out there is hard. We do it because it works, not because we love it. The more you stand out in a crowd, the more likely someone who needs you on their team will reach out to you. Every time you pen a blog, upload a video, contribute to a white paper, or join an industry panel, you increase the likelihood that one of those 100 souls out of 900 million will find you.

Keep your content consistent. It will get better with time. You don't have to write Pulitzer Prize-winning articles and you don't have to have the video production of someone with millions of subscribers, you just have to share authentic, helpful content that makes people see you as an authority on a topic. Try writing simple posts at first, and then maybe some articles. Or you could try posting short videos (like my Instagram Reels). Find what is comfortable for you and just be consistent in delivering that content.

You don't even have to FEEL like an authority. Imposter syndrome is very real at this level. Focus on talking to someone less experienced than you in your content. If you put good, consistent content out there, it will do its job for you automagically.

Outbound: reaching out to executives

This is where we get a little ninja-like in our approach. We want to stand out in a crowd, but not in a way that reduces our executive presence. We also want to stand out in a way that doesn't look like we have something to sell. Marketers have ruined everything, including LinkedIn connection requests.

Actual outreach methods

Generally speaking the best way to reach out to executives are through LinkedIn messages, email, and phone calls. Sure you can send a hand-written note or a personalized video and stuff like that, but never in cold outreach the first time you're trying to connect with them.

LinkedIn messaging. This depends on how active the executive is on LinkedIn. When you go to their profile, you will see a section called "Activity". You can tell if someone has been active on LinkedIn by looking here. If the last thing they've done was 9 months ago it will appear blank and you can assume they won't be responsive. If they are active on LinkedIn, your job now is to connect with them and not look like a spammer, marketer, or job applicant.

  • If you're a first-level connection, awesome, just send them a message.
  • If you're a second-level connection, it depends on several factors but you can either directly message them, or you can send a connection request, and in doing so you can write a brief message.
  • If you're a third-level connection, try to see if they're in any LinkedIn groups that you're also a part of, or join some groups they're in as well. You can message people on LinkedIn even if they're a 3rd-level connection if you're in the same group.

Email. Beyond LinkedIn, email is still the king of connectivity for executives. Most executives haven't adopted DM apps like Instagram, Discord, Snap, etc. So for them email is going to be the most expected way for someone to connect with them. You can usually find someone's email address either on LinkedIn or the company website. You can also try to guess their email by following a corporate email standard (tcox@vmleader.com, tom.cox@vmleader.com, tomcox@vmleader.com, etc.)

You can send maybe two, and MAYBE three emails at most. I wouldn't send more than two without a response. Again marketers ruin everything, and marketers teach a method of sending what looks like multiple ignored emails in a single email, causing instant guilt in the recipient, triggering a response. This is not the state of mind you want a hiring manager in when they respond to you.

Phone. Boomers, Gen-Xers, and older Millennials will likely be comfortable taking occasional phone calls. But they'll also be just fine to listen to (or read) a voicemail that you leave. I actually like to combine email and phone together. When I need to talk to someone important who has no idea who I am, I will call them, expect to go to voicemail, leave a VM and tell them in the VM that I'm also following up with an email. That way if they don't see my email they'll look for it in spam (if they want to read what I wrote that is).

Other methods. You could try writing a physical letter that is sent via post if that is your style and you believe a particular executive would react well to this. But this can cut two ways. It will either come off as novel and genuine, or it will look like you're trying too hard. You want to be slightly in demand and hand-writing a letter can come off as either disingenuous or desperate. I'd rather not take the chances unless I know for certain a particular executive reacts well to those sorts of things. If you have tried LinkedIn messaging, email, and phone call / voicemail and haven't heard from them, it's really not wise to go any further than that. They either don't want to talk to you, don't care enough to talk to you, don't have enough time to talk to you, or for whatever other reason they are not going to talk to you. Let it go and move on to the next person.

What to say when you reach out

Generally speaking, when you do reach out, you want to offer value and be curious without seeming desperate or over-the-top.

  • Leverage mutual connections. It goes without saying if you have a strong enough mutual connection that can introduce you, burn that match first because this will be the fastest way to establish credibility.
  • Offer value first. One of the best ways to get the attention of an executive in a company is to offer something of value to them without any strings attached.
  • Congratulate them. Start out by referencing a recent company achievement, article or presentation they've been involved with. This shows you've done your homework and are genuinely interested in building a connection with them.
  • Connect them with a potential customer. Every executive loves sales. Even the ones who hate sales love it when the company gets new sales opportunities. If you are aware of a potential customer for their business, make an offer to connect them with someone you know could benefit from their product/service. Make sure it's a legit fit and a real opportunity.
  • Share a white paper / case study. If you've written a white paper or a case study for your industry (something I highly recommend for your thought leadership), offer to share it with them. Make it a link to your LinkedIn profile where you keep the document in your Media section, so they may connect with you after reading the document. Bonus if you get them to add value to the paper by sharing their insight with you.
  • Request advice. Someone once told me if you want advice, ask for a job. If you want a job, ask for advice. Executives often appreciate being approached for their expertise. Frame your message as seeking advice or feedback on a particular industry challenge or trend, rather than just directly seeking a job.

III. Tools and scripts for outreach

I recommend using these scripts to reach out to executives and recruiters, as well as these tracking tools (or whatever works best for you) in order to keep track of all of your outreach. It is up to YOU to track these things. The things you measure are the things that improve, so measure your outreach efforts and you'll see improvement.

Connecting with recruiters: the "Knock-Knock" method

With this approach, you are asking the recruiter at a company to connect to your network. It's a little less direct than "OMG HIRE ME PLS."

Hi Jacki,

I'm also a huge Atlanta Braves fan and am excited to watch how far they go this fall. I saw that you have an opening on your design team. As a Sr. Design Engineer, I'm happy to share why I'd be a perfect fit.

Would you be open to joining my LinkedIn network in the meantime?

Thanks!

Connecting with executives

Hello [Executive's Name],

I've followed [Company's Name] for some time and admire its contributions to [specific industry trait or innovation]. I'm currently in touch with [Client's Company Name], who might benefit from [Company's product/service/solution]. I thought it could be a valuable introduction for [Company's Name].

Alongside, I've been contemplating my next steps in [industry], and I'm deeply interested in the work [Company's Name] is leading. It would be an honor to connect and discuss both opportunities.

Warm regards,
[Your Name]

Connecting with a potential customer #2 (casual & direct)

Hey [Executive's Name],

Hope you're doing well! I've been tracking [Company's Name]'s journey, and kudos to what you all have achieved. I've got someone in my network, [Client's Company Name], that might be right up your alley for [specific product/service]. Thought it would be helpful to you to connect over that.

By the way, I've been on the lookout for new challenges in our industry, and [Company's Name] keeps coming up on my radar. Mind if we chat about it?

Cheers,
[Your Name]

Connecting with a potential customer #3 (formal & detailed)

Dear [Executive's Name],

I trust this message finds you well. Over the years, I've developed a profound respect for [Company's Name] and its impact on [specific industry aspect]. I represent a connection with [Client's Company Name], which, I believe, aligns seamlessly with the offerings of [Company's Name]. An introduction might prove mutually beneficial.

In parallel, my professional aspirations have led me to consider opportunities that align with the ethos of [Company's Name]. I would be most grateful for an opportunity to discuss this further.

Yours sincerely,
[Your Name]

Connecting with a potential customer #4 (enthusiastic & energetic)

Hi [Executive's Name],

Wow! [Company's Name]'s recent [project/product launch] was a game-changer! Major applause from this side. Speaking of exciting things, I've been in talks with [Client's Company Name], and there's a buzz that they're on the lookout for exactly what you offer. Let's connect over this?

And, not to switch gears too fast, but I'm super charged up about possibly bringing my skills to a place like [Company's Name]. Think we could discuss it?

Stay awesome,
[Your Name]

Connecting with a report or white paper

Dear [Executive's Name],

I hope this finds you well. Over the past few months, I've been delving deep into the nuances of our industry and have compiled a comprehensive report detailing some emerging trends and insights that might be of interest to [Company's Name].

Given your position and [Company's Name]'s influential role in the sector, I thought you might find this research beneficial. I'd be delighted to share it with you and receive any feedback or insights you might have.

On a parallel note, I've been keenly observing the strides [Company's Name] is making in our industry, and I am genuinely interested in discussing potential avenues where my expertise might complement your team's visionary efforts.

Would you be open to connecting?

Warm regards,
[Your Name]

Outreach tracking toolkit

Keeping track of your network outreach will help you stay on top of the process. Over the course of this process, you will interact with hundreds of people. It only takes one connection to make it all worthwhile, so stay organized and focused on making sure you maximize your interactions with each person in your new executive network. There are two ways to approach tracking your outreach: a spreadsheet or a Kanban board. Either way will keep you relatively organized. Just depends on your personality and technical acumen on which is preferable to you.

Spreadsheet

The old-school, simple way is to use a spreadsheet. You can use one in Google Sheets, Microsoft Excel, or other programs like Apple Numbers, Notion, and so on. I have provided both a Google Sheets version you can copy as well as an Excel version you can download.

Kanban board

The way I like to track interactions in general, whether they're with customers or for tracking job opportunities, is to use a Kanban board. Kanban boards are simple but very powerful tools, they let you create a "card" for a task or some other object, and move it left to right from one category to another. You start by creating tasks that go into "To Do", move them to "Doing" when you're actually doing them, and then move them to "Done" when you've completed them.

Most Client Relationship Management (CRM) software tools come with Kanban boards that you can reorganize to match your specific workflow. My personal favorite Kanban software is called Trello. I start with tasks in the "To Do" section, move them to "Today" in the morning, move them to "Doing" when I'm actively working on them, put them in the "Parking Lot" if I'm waiting for someone else to do something before I can finish the task, and then finally in the "Done" column once they've been completed. The software makes it super-easy to create a card, fill out all sorts of information on them, color-code them, assign people, set dates, and so on.

When setting up a Kanban board for tracking job opportunities, you can create your own board, but Trello also has a template library, and one of the templates is a Job Hunt template that has a pre-populated template that can get you started quickly. The default categories are set up for someone who is using old-school tactics for applying for jobs on job boards like LinkedIn and Indeed. I like to modify the columns to better suit our needs. So I would include the following categories:

  • Targeted Organizations: list companies you are interested in pursuing and add names here as you find them. On the individual cards include the names & contact info of hiring managers and other influencers.
  • Connected: move the card to here once you've connected with a hiring manager or executive in the targeted company.
  • Role Defined: here's where you put a card when there's a clear role defined by them that is either built specifically for you, or is an opening to which you can apply.
  • Applied: once you've applied to the role move it here.
  • Interview: when you're interviewing move the card here. You can color-code your cards to indicate which interview you are on. At the executive level, 2-3 interviews are common, 4-5 slightly less common. More than 5 or only one would be a red flag.
  • Offers: list your offers here.

Inside of each card, you can keep a running tab of activities that are happening with that opportunity. If you're comfortable with Kanban boards from CRMs, or you want to try out Trello (I LOVE this tool) give this a go, but the spreadsheet works just as well if you are not a highly visual person.
